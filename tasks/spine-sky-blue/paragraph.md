# Sky-blue, stage 2 (paragraph)

**Title:** Why is the sky blue at noon and red at sunset?
**Setup:** It's the same sky and the same Sun.

**Paragraph:**
[1] The Sun looks yellow, but its light is really all colours mixed together. Air looks clear, but it isn't quite. So how does clear air turn sunlight into a blue sky, and later a red and orange sunset? [2] Light travels in waves, and each colour has its own length. Blue waves are short and red waves are long. Air is made of molecules, tiny bits of gas far too small to see. They scatter light, which means they send it off in all directions. [3] Air scatters blue about four times as strongly as red, so far more blue than red is sent off. That scattered blue reaches your eyes from all over the sky. The sky has no blue of its own. The blue was in the sunlight all along. [4] At sunset the Sun is low, so its light slants through far more air. That is about 38 times as much as when the Sun is straight overhead. On the way, much of the blue is scattered off in other directions and never reaches you. Red and orange are what is left. The Sun looks yellow at noon for the same reason. The air has already scattered some blue out of its light. The same scattering that makes the sky blue takes the blue out of the sunset.

**Slide most likely to lose a reader:** [3] ("four times as strongly" and the idea that the blue was already in the sunlight), then [4] (a low Sun means a longer path through the air).

## Notes
- **Slide 1 is your wording with two changes:** "Sunlight looks yellow" became "The Sun looks yellow" (sunlight is white; only the Sun looks yellow, claims 1 and 10), and "later an orange one" became "later a red and orange sunset" (the title says red, and only the sky near the Sun goes orange; sheet section 1).
- **Cold reader fixes:** "shorter" (now "short" and "long" with "each colour has its own length"); "isn't quite" clear left as your wording, the reader found it odd and the paragraph does not explain it; "scatter" used for both the blue sky and the missing blue at sunset, now "scattered off in other directions and never reaches you"; why a low Sun means a longer path ("slants through"); the Sun's colour loose end (the yellow Sun line).
- **Open for the cold read:** where the scattered blue goes at sunset (not on the sheet as a safe claim), and "four times as strongly" (what "strongly" measures).
