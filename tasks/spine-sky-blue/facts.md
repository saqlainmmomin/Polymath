# Fact sheet: Why is the sky blue at noon and red at sunset?

Checked 2026-10-30. Pages opened with WebFetch (which returns the quoted lines through a summariser, so quotes are as returned; spot-check before printing any in copy): Met Office "Why the sky is blue and sunsets red"; NASA Space Place "Why Is the Sky Blue?"; NASA Goddard "What Color is the Sun??"; NASA Science "Visible Light"; NASA Mars sunset page; NASA Earth facts; NOAA Storm Prediction Centre, Corfidi, "The Colors of Sunset and Twilight"; Atmospheric Optics (atoptics.co.uk) "Why is the Sky Blue?"; UC Riverside Physics FAQ (Baez) "Why is the sky blue?"; UCAR/NCAR SciEd "Blue Skies and Red Sunsets"; Scientific American "Sunset Colors in a Glass"; Met Office "Why are clouds white?"; ISTA Austria "The origin of ocean blue"; Wikipedia (Air mass (astronomy), read as raw wikitext with curl, so exact; Rayleigh scattering, Sunset, Atmosphere of Earth, read through the summariser). Not found or weak: hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu failed on a certificate error, and hyperphysics.gsu.edu gave no ratio; Britannica returned 403; the NOAA Western Region "skyblue" archive page returned empty; no source opened states outright that the sky overhead stays blue while the sunset glows (medium, claim 11 note); the "milk demo needs an incandescent torch" point comes from a search summary only.

Scope: a clear day, sea level, one observer. Not in scope: twilight after the Sun sets, Rayleigh maths beyond the 1/wavelength^4 rule, polarisation, the green flash, other planets (except one Go-deeper line).

## 1. Is the title literally true?
- **"The sky is blue at noon."** True on a clear day, and it is blue all day, not only at noon (Corfidi: "The familiar blue of the daytime sky is the result of the selective scattering of sunlight by air molecules"). It is not true of every sky: "Hazy daytime skies, instead of being bright blue, appear grayish or even white" (Corfidi); clouds are white (Met Office, claim in section 4). Near the horizon the sky is paler even on a clear day (see the end of this section).
- **"Red at sunset."** Both red and orange, and sometimes yellow, depending on the air. Met Office: "we see the longer wavelengths - reds and oranges". Corfidi: "the light that reaches an observer early or late in the day is noticeably reddened". Baez Physics FAQ: "When the air is clear the sunset will appear yellow ... If the air is polluted with small particles, natural or otherwise, the sunset will be more red." Wikipedia (Sunset): "leaving the longer wavelength orange and red hues". So "red" is true as a loose word, "red and orange" is exact. **The title says red, the owner's slide 1 says orange: pick one pair of words and use it everywhere. Recommended: "red and orange" in the slides, title untouched (owner's wording).**
- **What the title assumes that is not always true:**
  - A clear sky. Cloud and haze change both colours (above). Sunset colour depends on the air: Corfidi says particles "subdue" colours ("Clean air is, in fact, the main ingredient common to brightly colored sunrises and sunsets"), while Wikipedia says "evening air contains more particles" and so sunsets are "more brilliant", and Baez says polluted air gives "more red". Sources disagree on particles, so say nothing about pollution making sunsets better or worse.
  - "Noon" means "Sun high up". Outside the tropics the Sun is never straight overhead. My calculation: at noon at 51.5 degrees north (London) the Sun is about 62 degrees up at midsummer and about 15 degrees up at midwinter, so the air it crosses is only 1.1 or 3.8 times the overhead amount (Kasten-Young formula, from Wikipedia Air mass). Any "times more air" figure must be set against "straight overhead", not against "noon".
  - "The sky turns red at sunset" read as the whole sky. It is the sky near the Sun and the Sun itself that go orange and red. Wikipedia (Sunset) says the overhead sky receives blue "scattered from the surrounding sky"; medium, see claim 11. Do not let a picture colour the whole sky orange.
  - Sunrise does the same thing. Fine to say "sunset" only.
- **At noon, what does a reader see near the horizon?** A paler, whiter blue, even on a clear day. NASA Space Place: "Closer to the horizon, the sky fades to a lighter blue or white." Atmospheric Optics: "Near the horizon the air path is 10X or more longer. Along it, photons are scattered several times and the reds and greens eventually become as strong as blues to yield white." Haze near the ground adds to it (not sourced, so keep it out).
- **Is "same Sun, same sky" literally true?** The Sun: yes, it is the same Sun, and the light leaving it is the same (NASA: white from space). The sky: yes if "sky" means the same air above you. What changes is the angle: at sunset the Sun's light crosses far more air to reach you. Not literally true to say the sky itself "turns" orange, since only the part near the Sun does (above). The opener is fine as a hook, with that one exception.
- **Best:** keep the owner's title. Literal truth rests on claims 8 and 9 and on the clear-day condition.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
Sunlight is a mix of colours, and the molecules in air scatter blue light about four times as strongly as red, so on a clear day scattered blue arrives from all over the sky, while at sunset the Sun's light crosses so much more air that much of its blue has been scattered away before it reaches you, leaving red and orange.

## 3. Safe claims
1. Sunlight looks white but is a mix of all the colours of the rainbow.
   *high. Met Office: "Sunlight, although it looks white, is actually made up of many colours, each with a different wavelength." NASA Science, Visible Light: "As the full spectrum of visible light travels through a prism, the wavelengths separate into the colors of the rainbow because each color is a different wavelength." https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/optical-effects/why-is-the-sky-blue ; https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/. Note: Met Office says sunlight looks white, the owner's slide 1 says it looks yellow (see claim 10 and the summary).*
2. Each colour of light is a wave with its own length, and blue waves are shorter than red waves.
   *high. NASA Space Place: "Blue light waves are shorter than red light waves." NASA Science: visible light runs from about 380 nanometres (violet, shortest) to 700 nanometres (red, longest); blue is about 450. Do not print "nanometre" in copy. https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/ ; https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/*
3. Air is made of gas molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and they are too small to see.
   *high. NASA Earth facts: "78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, and 1% other gases" near the surface (https://science.nasa.gov/earth/facts/). Met Office: "tiny gas molecules - mainly nitrogen and oxygen". Individual molecules are far smaller than a wavelength of light (Corfidi: "Air molecules are much smaller than this --- about a thousand times smaller", on the particle-size line for good scattering; https://spc.noaa.gov/publications/corfidi/sunset), so "too small to see" is safe.*
4. These molecules are enough to scatter sunlight into a blue sky; dust is not needed.
   *high. Baez Physics FAQ: scientists "supposed correctly that the molecules of oxygen and nitrogen in the air are sufficient to account for the scattering" (dust would have made the colour vary much more with humidity and haze than is seen). https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/physics/General/BlueSky/blue_sky.html. Corfidi: "The familiar blue of the daytime sky is the result of the selective scattering of sunlight by air molecules."*
5. Scattering sends light off in new directions, and air scatters blue light more strongly than red, about four times as strongly for the blue and red used here.
   *high for the rule, medium-high for the number. The rule: scattering is "inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength" (Atmospheric Optics; Baez), so a shorter wave scatters far more. The number depends on which two colours are compared: Atmospheric Optics gives "blue light of 450nm wavelength is scattered 4.4X more strongly than 650nm red light" (https://www.atoptics.co.uk/atoptics/blsky.htm). My arithmetic from the same rule (650/450 to the 4th = 4.35): blue 450 against red 700 gives 5.9, blue 470 against red 650 gives 3.7, violet 400 against red 700 gives 9.4. Corfidi gives "three to four times" for violet against "longer wavelengths" in general (a different comparison). So the number is "a blue of 450 and a red of 650": say "about four times as strongly" and keep it only as that pair. Never print "5 times" or "4.4" without the pair; never print the 1/fourth-power rule.*
6. The scattered light goes off in all directions, so scattered blue reaches your eyes from every part of the sky.
   *high. NASA Space Place: "Blue light is scattered in all directions by the tiny molecules of air in Earth's atmosphere." "Every part" is safe; "wherever you look" is not, because of the paler horizon and the glare around the Sun (section 1). Say "from all over the sky", and see claim 12.*
7. The sky has no blue of its own; the blue is sunlight, scattered.
   *high. Corfidi (above). Space has no air and is black (EarthDate episode title "Space is black - so why is the sky blue...", http://www.earthdate.org/episodes/blue-sky-blue-sunset).*
8. Violet light is scattered even more than blue, but sunlight has less violet in it and our eyes are less sensitive to violet, so the sky looks blue, not violet.
   *high. Met Office: "Even though violet light is scattered even more than blue, our eyes are more sensitive to blue, and there's less violet in sunlight to begin with." Atmospheric Optics: "The sky is not violet because sunlight is weaker in violet compared to blue and the eye is less sensitive to it." UCAR agrees on the eye. Two sources add that the upper atmosphere absorbs some violet (Baez; Wikipedia); left out as not agreed by the Met Office. Do not say violet is "missing"; it is in the scattered light (section 4).*
9. When the Sun is low, its light crosses far more air to reach you, and much of the blue in it is scattered out of the direct beam, so red and orange are what is left.
   *high. Met Office: "At sunrise and sunset, the Sun is low in the sky, so its light passes through a thicker layer of atmosphere. The shorter wavelength blue light is scattered further ... and we see the longer wavelengths - reds and oranges." Corfidi: "Because this lengthened path results in an increased amount of violet and blue light being scattered out of the beam ... the light that reaches an observer early or late in the day is noticeably reddened." Wikipedia (Sunset): blue and green are "removed almost completely". UCAR says "Only the red light comes through", which is too strong; use "much of the blue" and "red and orange are left".*
10. The Sun looks yellow from the ground because the air has already scattered some blue out of its light; seen from above the air, the Sun looks white.
    *high. NASA Goddard: "If we were above the atmosphere, say on the International Space Station and looked at the sun (through our filtered visor), the sun would appear white!" and "Since shorter wavelength blue light is scattered more efficiently than longer wavelength red light, we lose some of the blue tint of the sun as sunlight passes through the atmosphere." https://science.gsfc.nasa.gov/attic/eclipse2017.gsfc.nasa.gov/what-color-sun.html. This is the "yellow Sun" of slide 1, and it is the same scattering as the sky.*
11. A Sun on the horizon has about 38 times as much air in front of it as a Sun straight overhead.
    *medium-high. Wikipedia, Air mass (astronomy), raw text: "reaching a value of approximately 38 at the horizon"; the Kasten-Young fit gives "approximately 38 at the horizon"; the spherical-atmosphere model gives 38.87 and "the horizon air mass in the more realistic spherical atmosphere is usually less than 40". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_mass_(astronomy). Conditions, all to be set in the sentence: sea level, standard air, the Sun's edge at the horizon, compared with straight overhead. The same page warns real values near the horizon vary with temperature, pressure and haze ("accurate calculation of air mass near the horizon is all but impossible"), so say "about 38", not "38.9". At a Sun 5 degrees up the factor is about 10 (my calculation, Kasten-Young), so the effect is large well before the Sun touches the horizon. Safe plain form: "about 38 times as much air as when the Sun is straight overhead". Optional, and if the figure feels risky, drop it and write "far more air" (needs no number). The sky overhead at sunset staying blue rests on a single secondary line (Wikipedia, Sunset: the overhead sky receives blue "scattered from the surrounding sky"); not a claim here, since no primary source was opened.*
12. Near the horizon the sky is paler, because you look through much more air and the light is scattered several times.
    *high. NASA Space Place and Atmospheric Optics, quoted in section 1 ("10X or more longer"). Keep "paler", never "white" alone.*

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"The sky is blue because it reflects the ocean" (or the ocean is blue from the sky).** False. ISTA: "You often hear that the blue ocean reflects merely the blue sky. But that's not true." The sky is blue over a desert. Safe replacement: skip it; the sky's blue is sunlight scattered by the air (claim 7).
- **"Air is blue."** Air is not blue by itself; a thin amount looks clear. The blue is sunlight scattered by it (claim 7). Safe replacement: "Air looks clear, but it scatters a little light, and the whole sky adds up to blue."
- **"Dust (or smoke, or water drops) makes the sky blue."** False. Molecules are enough (claim 4). Haze makes the sky grey or white (Corfidi), and clouds are white because droplets "scatter all wavelengths with roughly the same effectiveness" (Met Office, https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/types-of-weather/clouds/why-are-clouds-white). Safe replacement: the word "dust" never appears except when stating it is not needed.
- **"Scattering is the same as reflection."** Not safe. Reflection (a mirror, a ball off a wall) sends light one way; scattering sends it in all directions (NASA Space Place) and the molecule re-emits the light (UCAR: "scatter (re-emit)"). A reader who hears "bounce" or "reflect" may picture a mirror or a ball. Safe replacement: "scatter", defined once as "sends it off in all directions" (section 9).
- **"Violet is missing from sunlight / the sky."** False. It is in sunlight and scattered most of all; there is less of it and eyes see it less well (claim 8). Safe replacement: use claim 8 as written.
- **"Blue gets absorbed at sunset."** False. It is scattered out of the beam, off in other directions (Met Office, Corfidi: "scattered out of the beam"). Absorbed light would be gone; scattered blue lights the sky somewhere else. Safe replacement: "scattered away" or "knocked sideways".
- **"Sunsets are red because of pollution only."** False. A clean sky has red and orange sunsets; the colour comes from the long path (claim 9). Particles change how bright and how red (Baez: "more red"; Corfidi: "subdue"; sources disagree). Safe replacement: say nothing about pollution.
- **"The Sun gets closer / redder at sunset."** False. The Sun gives off the same light; the air in front of it changes it (claim 9; NASA: white from space).
- **"Red is not scattered at all."** False; red scatters too, only about four times less (claim 5). Safe replacement: "less".
- **"The whole sky is orange at sunset."** False; see section 1.
- **"The sky is blue everywhere you look."** Not at the horizon (claim 12), nor in clouds.
- **"The sky is blue because blue is the Sun's colour"** or **"blue because the Sun is yellow"** both false. The Sun is white from space (claim 10).

## 5. One good number
**About 38 times as much air** in front of a Sun on the horizon as in front of a Sun straight overhead (claim 11, medium-high; conditions in the claim). Everyday comparison, derived by me, not printed by any single source: if all the air above you were pressed to the thickness it has at ground level it would be about 8.4 km deep (Wikipedia Air mass, "y_atm approximately 8435 m"); 38 times that is about 330 km, so the Sun's light at sunset crosses air about as thick as a short walk (5 miles) multiplied into a half-day drive (200 miles). **Medium: the 8.4 km is an equivalent height, a difficult idea, so the comparison is optional.** The plainer option is to drop the comparison and let the picture of a long slanted path against a short straight one carry it. Alternative number, if the owner prefers the other half of the story: blue light of one colour is scattered about 4.4 times as strongly as a red (claim 5), but only with the pair named.

## 6. Analogies, judged
- **A glass of water with a few drops of milk, lit with a torch (the classic classroom demo).** Right for both halves in one picture: from the side the lit water looks blue, and the light that has crossed the whole glass looks yellow, orange or red. UCAR/NCAR: "The light will look blue where it has traveled through milky water ... where the light has traveled through most of the milky water, the light appears yellow, orange, or red"; Scientific American: the milk is "an emulsion ... many tiny insoluble fat particles"; UCAR: "The suspended particles of milk scatter light like molecules and other particles in Earth's atmosphere." https://scied.ucar.edu/activity/blue-skies-and-red-sunsets ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/sunset-colors-in-a-glass/. The obvious next thought is "so the air is full of something like milk", which is the dust myth: milk drops are far bigger than gas molecules. It also works only with a warm-white incandescent torch (search-summary source only; low). **Safest of the three: use it in one line, say it is "a few drops of milk" standing in for the air, never "air is like milk".**
- **Ripples meeting small posts in a pond (short ripples knocked about more than long ones).** The direction is right, but it is untested here (no source opened). Misleads: a molecule is not a fixed post, the long ripples are not skipped altogether, and the reader pictures solid things hitting the light. **Not recommended.**
- **A stack of tinted glass sheets (more sheets, redder).** Right for "a longer path removes more blue". Misleads: tinted glass absorbs, air scatters, and the reader's next thought, "the blue is destroyed", is the absorbed-blue myth. **Don't use.**
- A rainbow or prism is not an analogy but a fact for claim 1 (NASA Science, prism). Pinball or ball bouncing: misleads (reflection; every colour alike). **Avoid.**
Recommendation: one analogy, the milk glass, or none (the picture of a long and a short path can carry it).

## 7. Go deeper thread
**Clouds are white because their drops are much bigger than air molecules, and they scatter all the colours alike.** *high — Met Office: "Bigger particles like water droplets within a cloud scatter all wavelengths with roughly the same effectiveness"; the sunlight "continues to remain white".* Optional second thread, a one-line follow-on: on Mars the sky near the setting Sun looks blue, because of very fine dust, not gas. *medium-high — NASA Mars sunset page: the "very fine dust is the right size so that blue light penetrates the atmosphere slightly more efficiently" (read through the summariser; check before printing).* A third, only if asked: where the Sun's light is red at sunset, the blue taken out of that beam is scattered sideways into the sky; the same scattering is on both ends. *medium-high — Corfidi: "Thus, it could be said that sunsets are red because the daytime sky is blue."*

## 9. Replacement words
- **What the light meets: "the air"** for the doing ("the air scatters blue light"); **"molecules"** once, glossed on first use ("tiny bits of gas, far too small to see"); never "particles", "dust", "gases" as a noun after that. Reason: "particles" brings in dust; "gas molecules" is the exact thing (claim 3).
- **"scatter"** (not "bounce", "reflect", "deflect", "disperse"). Define once: "scatter means sends it off in all directions" (NASA Space Place wording). Keep "scattered away" for the sunset beam. "Bounce" drops out because it suggests a ball or a mirror.
- **"colour"** (British), "colours", "coloured"; never "color".
- **"wave"** and **"short / long"**, never "wavelength", "frequency", "nanometre". "Blue waves are shorter than red waves."
- **"much of the blue"** (not "most", "all"); **"red and orange"** for the sunset (not "red" alone, not "reddish").
- **"far more air"** or **"about 38 times as much air as straight overhead"**; never "times more".
- **"about four times as strongly"** for blue against red, with the pair kept to the sheet.
- **"the Sun looks yellow"** (not "sunlight is yellow"); sunlight is white and the Sun looks yellow (claims 1 and 10).
- Banned: "Rayleigh", "Mie", "refraction", "spectrum", "inversely", "fourth power", "wavelength", "nanometre", "photon", "particles" (see above), "dust" (except as "not dust"), "reflect", "absorb" (blue is scattered, not absorbed), "usually", "mostly", "typically", "about 40 times more", "exactly", "always", "turns the sky red".

## One-line summary: what in the owner's slide 1 is not literally true
"Sunlight looks yellow" is not literally true (Met Office: sunlight "looks white"; only the Sun looks yellow, because the air has scattered some blue out of its light, and it is white from space), and "later an orange one" is true only of the sky near the setting Sun, not the whole sky; also the title says "red" while the slide says "orange", and the rest of the slide holds.
