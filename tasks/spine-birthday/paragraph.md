# Birthday, stage 2 (paragraph for owner approval)

**Title:** In a room of 23, what's the chance two share a birthday?
**Setup:** Take a guess before you read on.

**Paragraph:**
[1] Many people guess a small chance, but it's about 50%. A true fact that sounds wrong is called a paradox, and this one is the birthday paradox. [2] It feels wrong because we think about our own birthday. Walk into a room of 22 strangers, and the chance someone shares your birthday is less than 6%. [3] But the match doesn't have to include you. If all 23 of you shook hands once with each other, there'd be 253 handshakes. Each handshake is a pair who could share a birthday, and only 22 of them include you. [4] Any one pair almost never matches. But more pairs means a higher chance of a match, and 253 pairs push it to about 50%. [5] Each new person makes a new pair with everyone already in the room, so pairs pile up fast. By 57 people there are 1,596 pairs, and the chance of a match is over 99%.

**Bold line:** Count the pairs, not the people.

**Go deeper:** At the 2014 football World Cup, each squad had 23 players, and about half the squads had two players with the same birthday. A neat twist: to get a better-than-half chance that a stranger shares your birthday, you'd need 253 strangers, the same number as the pairs in a room of 23. That's no accident: either way it's 253 pairs. Real rooms match a little more often than this, because births aren't spread evenly across the year. Check the next team or class you see.

**Slide most likely to lose a reader:** [4]. It says one pair almost never matches and 253 pairs give just over half, but doesn't show how; the picture (rooms of 23 dealt onto a calendar, a tally settling near half) has to carry that step, and the text must never put "1 in 365" beside "253", or readers divide and get 69%.
