# Fact sheet: How does your brain bring back a memory?

## 1. Is the title literally true?
Yes. "Bringing back a memory" is what memory scientists call recall (or retrieval): getting something you stored earlier back into your mind. The brain really does the work, with the hippocampus (a small curled part deep in each side of the brain) helping to pull the pieces together for memories of events. Two limits for the copy: this topic is about memories of things that happened to you and facts you learned, not skills like riding a bike, which come back in a different way; and the topic must stand alone, so it should say in one line that a memory is a lasting change in how brain cells are connected, left by something you experienced or learned.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
A memory is not kept in one place as a recording; it is spread across many linked groups of brain cells, and when a cue (a question, a smell, a song, a place) wakes up part of that pattern, the activity spreads to the linked parts until the whole memory is rebuilt, with the brain filling any gaps from what it knows and expects, which is why memories can change without you noticing.

## 3. Safe claims
1. A memory of an event is not stored as one complete recording in one spot; its parts (what you saw, heard, felt) are held in different areas of the brain, and the hippocampus helps link them together.
   *high — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17696170/ (Teyler & Rudy 2007, "The hippocampal indexing theory and episodic memory", Hippocampus); https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK10915/ (Purves, Neuroscience, textbook)*
2. Recall usually starts with a cue: something in the present, like a question, a smell, a song or a place, that matches part of what was stored.
   *high — https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1974-03302-001 (Tulving & Thomson 1973, encoding specificity, Psychological Review)*
3. When a cue wakes up part of a stored pattern, activity spreads through the connections to the rest of it, so a small piece can bring back the whole memory; scientists call this pattern completion.
   *high — https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnsys.2013.00074/full (Rolls 2013, "The mechanisms for pattern completion and pattern separation in the hippocampus", Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience)*
4. Cues work best when they match how things were when you first learned them: in a classic test, divers who learned words underwater remembered more of them underwater, and those who learned on land remembered more on land.
   *high — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1975.tb01468.x (Godden & Baddeley 1975, British Journal of Psychology)*
5. Remembering is rebuilding, not replaying: each time, the brain puts the memory together again from its stored pieces and fills the gaps with what it knows and expects.
   *high — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17395575/ (Schacter & Addis 2007, "The cognitive neuroscience of constructive memory", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B); Bartlett 1932, Remembering (Cambridge University Press)*
6. Because of this, a memory can change without you noticing, for example when you later hear new details or leading words about the event.
   *high — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1183925/ (Loftus 2005, "Planting misinformation in the human mind: a 30-year investigation", Learning & Memory)*
7. In 1974, people watched films of car crashes and were asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" or "hit" each other; the "smashed" group guessed about 41 mph on average, the "hit" group about 34 mph.
   *high — Loftus & Palmer 1974, Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior 13:585–589 (smashed 40.8, collided 39.3, bumped 38.1, hit 34.0, contacted 31.8 mph; 45 students); summary with the table: https://www.simplypsychology.org/loftus-palmer.html*
8. In a second test a week later, about 1 in 3 people who had heard "smashed" said they had seen broken glass, against about 1 in 7 who had heard "hit", though there was no broken glass in the film.
   *high — Loftus & Palmer 1974, Experiment 2 (150 students: smashed 16 of 50 = 32%, hit 7 of 50 = 14%, no question about speed 6 of 50 = 12%); https://www.simplypsychology.org/loftus-palmer.html*
9. Feeling very sure about a memory does not prove it is right: students who wrote down how they heard about 9/11 the next day later changed their stories as much as for an ordinary day, yet stayed just as sure of them.
   *high — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12930476/ (Talarico & Rubin 2003, "Confidence, not consistency, characterizes flashbulb memories", Psychological Science)*
10. Often a memory you cannot bring back is still stored, and what is missing is the right cue; the feeling of a word being "on the tip of your tongue" is this, and most people get it about once a week.
    *high — https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1991-24963-001 (A. S. Brown 1991, "A review of the tip-of-the-tongue experience", Psychological Bulletin); Brown & McNeill 1966; https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1974-03302-001 (Tulving & Thomson 1973)*
11. Picking the right answer from a list (recognition) is usually easier than coming up with it on your own (free recall), because the answers you see act as cues.
    *high — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK10915/ (Purves, Neuroscience); standard in any cognitive psychology textbook, e.g. Baddeley, Eysenck & Anderson, Memory (Psychology Press)*
12. Pulling a memory back on purpose makes it easier to recall later: students who tested themselves on a passage remembered 56% of it a week later, against 42% for students who reread it.
    *high — https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, "Test-enhanced learning", Psychological Science, Experiment 1)*

Notes for the writer:
- The owner's hook says the brain rebuilds a memory "from scratch". Not true as worded: it rebuilds from stored pieces. Say "rebuilds it each time" or "puts it back together each time", never "from scratch" (that implies nothing was kept).
- "Scattered fragments" (source card 2) is fine as a picture, but say the pieces are linked. Pieces with no links would not come back together.
- Reconsolidation (source card "it gets re-saved") is NOT a safe claim for humans. It is well shown in rats (Nader, Schafe & LeDoux 2000, https://www.nature.com/articles/35021052), but human evidence is mixed, and a flagship human study failed seven replication attempts (Hardwicke et al. 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4868433/; review: Elsey, Van Ast & Kindt 2018, Psychological Bulletin, https://dare.uva.nl/id/dc53ddf8-3f2b-415a-bd16-49f5b5a587bf). The safe version for the copy: "each time you remember something, what you remember now can become part of the memory" (claim 6). Do not say the memory is "unstable", "deleted" or "re-saved" as a fact. If the owner wants the idea, at most: "Scientists are still testing an idea that..." — but better to drop it.
- Claim 9 (confidence): don't go further to "confidence means nothing". Under good conditions, a witness's first, quick confidence does track accuracy (Wixted & Wells 2017, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100616686966). The plain true line is "feeling sure is not proof".
- Claims 7 and 8: say "about 41 / 34 mph" and "1 in 3 / 1 in 7". The glass result is the better surprise. Don't say people "invented a crash" or that "most people" saw glass; two-thirds of the "smashed" group did not.
- Claim 12: the testing effect only wins after a delay. Five minutes later, rereaders did slightly better (81% vs 75%). Say "a week later".
- Claim 10's "once a week" is from a review of diary studies; it is an average, so say "about once a week" or "most people, often".

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"Your brain stores memories like a video you can play back."** False. Memories are rebuilt from pieces each time (claims 1, 5).
- **"Your brain rebuilds every memory from scratch."** Fix: it rebuilds from stored pieces, filling only the gaps.
- **"Each memory sits in one brain cell, or in one spot."** False. It is a pattern spread over many cells and areas.
- **"Every time you remember, the memory is erased and saved again" (reconsolidation as settled fact).** Not shown in humans; contested. Drop or flag as unproven (see notes).
- **"Vivid or confident memories are accurate."** False as a rule. Vividness and certainty can stay high while details drift (claim 9).
- **"If you can't remember it, it's gone."** Often false. Many "forgotten" memories come back with the right cue (claim 10).
- **"Hypnosis can unlock true hidden memories."** False. Hypnosis raises confidence and the number of recalled details, including wrong ones (American Psychological Association; Lynn et al. reviews).
- **"Loftus & Palmer showed people remembered a whole fake crash."** No. The crash was real in the film; the wording changed speed guesses and made some people report glass that wasn't there.
- **"Rereading your notes is the best way to remember."** No; testing yourself beats rereading after a delay (claim 12).
- **"We only use 10% of our brain."** Myth; don't let it slip in as a hook for "hidden memories".

## 5. One good number
A week after seeing a film of a car crash, about 1 in 3 people who had been asked how fast the cars "smashed" into each other said they remembered broken glass, against about 1 in 7 of those asked about cars that "hit" each other. There was no broken glass. One word, asked once, doubled the false memory. Everyday comparison: it is like two friends describing the same small bump in a car park, one says "they slammed into him", the other "they tapped him", and a week later you remember glass on the ground only if you heard "slammed".
*Source: Loftus & Palmer 1974, Experiment 2 (16 of 50 vs 7 of 50; 6 of 50 for people not asked about speed); https://www.simplypsychology.org/loftus-palmer.html. Solid as a classic, widely replicated in spirit (misinformation effect, Loftus 2005). Caveat: one study with 150 US students; say "in one famous experiment", not "people always".*

Backup number: tested students remembered 56% a week later, rereaders 42% (Roediger & Karpicke 2006). Use one number only.

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **A video recording.** Rejected, except as the thing it is NOT. Good to open with ("remembering isn't pressing play"), then drop.
- **A jigsaw with missing pieces that you finish from the box picture.** Right intuition for rebuilding plus gap-filling: you put the pieces back together and guess the gaps from what the scene "should" look like. Misleads if read as the pieces being loose in a box; they are linked, and pulling one tugs the others. Usable with "linked pieces".
- **Pulling one grape and the whole bunch comes up.** Right intuition for a cue plus pattern completion: grab one part, and the connected parts come with it. Misleads only slightly (grapes don't change); pair it with the jigsaw for the "rebuilt, can drift" part. Usable for the cue card.
- **A game of telephone (Chinese whispers) with yourself.** Right intuition for memories drifting each time they're retold. Misleads by implying every recall makes it worse and that drift happens only by retelling; many memories stay mostly right. Use carefully, say "can drift", not "always gets worse".
