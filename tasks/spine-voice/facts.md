# Fact sheet: Why does your voice sound different on a recording?

## 1. Is the title literally true?
Yes for "different", with three limits the copy must respect.
- **Different, not "higher".** The old textbook line is "the recording sounds higher". That is contested. A 2025 abstract found most participants believed their recorded voice sounded *lower*, the opposite of that prediction (source in section 3, note A). What is solid: the recording sounds thinner and less full than the voice you hear while speaking. The title says "different"; keep it that way and say "thinner" or "less full", never "higher".
- **The recording is not "wrong".** It is the voice other people hear: sound travelling through air from outside your head. It is your own live voice that is the odd one out, because it has an extra inside route. Say so plainly.
- **"Sounds that way to everyone else" is true for the route, not for every gadget.** Others hear you only through the air, like a microphone does. How close a given recording is depends on where the microphone sits and what plays it back (note B). The copy should say "reaches them the way the microphone gets it", not "the recording is exactly what they hear".

## 2. The answer in one sentence
When you talk, you hear yourself two ways, through the air and through the bones and flesh of your head, and the inside route adds fullness that a recording (and everyone else) never gets.

## 3. Safe claims
1. When you speak, sound reaches your ears by two routes: through the air into your ear canal, and as vibrations through the bones and flesh of your head.
   *high — https://kids.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frym.2025.1480846 (Frontiers for Young Minds, 2025: air conduction and bone conduction)*
2. Other people hear your voice only through the air, because the vibrations inside your head do not reach their ears.
   *high — https://scientificamerican.com/article/why-does-my-voice-sound-different*
3. A recording microphone sits outside your head, so it picks up only the air route.
   *high — https://kids.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frym.2025.1480846 ("only the air conduction part is recorded, because the microphone is outside your head")*
4. So a recording reaches you by the same route your friends hear you by: through the air, from outside your head.
   *high — https://scientificamerican.com/article/why-does-my-voice-sound-different (a recording leaves out the bone route you count as your "normal" voice; others hear only the air route). Do not upgrade to "sounds exactly like"; see section 1 and note B.*
5. The bones and flesh of your head carry the low, bassy part of a voice better than air does, so the inside route makes your voice sound fuller to you than to other people.
   *medium-high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_conduction ("the skull conducts lower frequencies better than air, people perceive their own voices to be lower and fuller") plus https://auditory.org/postings/2009/351.html ("sound transmission via bone conduction appears to be better at low frequencies"; hearing scientist's post) plus https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9929504 (Royal Society Open Science 2023: bone conduction "is assumed to instantiate a low-pass filter", i.e. lets lows through and cuts highs). Write "fuller" or "more bass". Do not write "deeper pitch"; see note A.*
6. A recording leaves out that bass-heavy inside route, so it sounds thinner to you than your voice does while you talk.
   *medium-high — https://auditory.org/postings/2009/351.html (recording has "more high frequency emphasis" and lacks the "low frequency weighted bone conducted sound"; this is why people call it "weak or thin") and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9929504 ("the lack of bone conduction that is inevitably present when hearing one's own voice while speaking")*
7. If you put your fingers in your ears and talk, your voice turns boomy and hollow, because the inside route gets stronger when the air route is blocked.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occlusion_effect ("sticking their fingers into their ears and talking"; "hollow" or "booming" sounds from your own voice) and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4197401 (Ear and Hearing 2014: blocking the ear canal amplifies bone-conducted sound; with the ear blocked, heartbeat became audible for most people)*
8. People are better at telling their own voice from someone else's when it is played through bone than through air.
   *high — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9929504 ("self-other voice discrimination improved for stimuli presented using bone as compared with air conduction")*
9. People who did not realise a recording was their own voice rated it as more attractive than other listeners did, so the dislike seems to come at least partly from knowing it is you.
   *medium-high — https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/09/130912112733.htm (Hughes and Harrison, Perception 2013: people "unknowingly assessed their own recorded voice as sounding more attractive in comparison to how others rated their voices"; authors also name liking the familiar). Keep the claim to "did not realise it was theirs"; the "because" half is the authors' reading, so the writer should drop "so..." if it would sound like proof.*
10. While you speak, your brain turns down its response to your own voice, because it already expects it.
   *medium — https://auditory.org/postings/2009/351.html ("auditory areas are suppressed in activity during speech production") and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11836694/ (2025: "speaking-induced suppression"). A "muscles/feedback" route was not verified; do not claim it.*
11. A recording gives you a version of your voice you rarely hear; only the air route is left, and it is the one everyone else gets of you.
   *high — https://scientificamerican.com/article/why-does-my-voice-sound-different ("in unfamiliar isolation")*

Dropped, not verified from an opened source: "muscle vibration/feedback adds to what you hear", "1967: only 38% recognise their own voice" (only seen on Wikipedia's Voice confrontation page, which I could not trace to the paper), "17.3% could not recognise their own voice" (task details unclear), the 80-person sample size of the 2013 study (seen only in a search snippet).

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"The recording makes you sound higher."** Contested. The only recent test I found (https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/view/4249, Canadian Acoustics abstract, AWC 2025) reports "most participants believe their recorded voices have been lowered in pitch, counter to a strong bone-conduction hypothesis", with social expectations and own-voice bias suspected. Say "thinner", "less full", "not like I sound to me".
- **"The skull makes your voice deeper."** Half true. It lets more of the low part through, which makes the voice fuller. The Royal Society paper says the exact filtering of the head "has not yet been formally defined", and a 22-person study (PMC11836694) found the pitch people feel as their own differs from the pitch the vocal cords make by about 0.9 of a semitone on average, with the direction varying person to person. So: "fuller", not "lower", and never "exactly".
- **"Bone conduction is the only reason."** Familiarity, expecting a different sound, and the way the brain quiets your own voice while you speak all plausibly play a part (claims 9, 10). Say "a big part", not "the reason".
- **"The recording is accurate, the way you really sound."** Too strong. It is the air-only version others get, but microphones and speakers colour it (note B). Safe: "the way your voice reaches other people".
- **"Cheap microphones are the cause."** Not supported. Even a good recording leaves out the inside route.
- **"You hear your voice only inside your head."** False. Claim 1: air route too.
- **"Other people hear you exactly as the recording."** See section 1 and note B.

## 5. One good number
None recommended. The only verified number is a small-study average (about 0.9 of a semitone, about one piano key, between the pitch the vocal cords make and the pitch people feel as theirs; 22 people, direction varied) from https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11836694/. It is a hard idea for a newcomer and invites the "higher/lower" myth, so skip it. Other numbers I saw (700 to 1200 Hz where the bone route dominates, Pörschmann 2000; 10 to 30 dB occlusion boost) mean nothing to a newcomer.

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **Fingers in your ears, then talk** (also a real experiment the reader can do now). *Safe.* Verified (claim 7). It makes the inside route easy to notice: voice turns boomy and hollow. Does not mislead if the copy says "you are blocking the air route, so the inside route stands out". Do not say it "turns the recording off".
- **Music through a wall versus in the room.** *Usable as a picture only; no claim from an opened source.* Intuition: the wall lets bass through and mutes the sparkle, like flesh and bone for the inside route. Misleads if the reader thinks the voice itself is "behind a wall" and the recording is the "real" one; the recording is simply the air-only version. Use words like "the inside route lets more of the bass through" and drop the wall unless a source is added.
- **Cup hands behind ears / blocked ears experiments.** Cupping hands is *not verified*; leave it out. Safe at-home tests from opened sources: (a) fingers in ears and talk (Wikipedia, Occlusion effect); (b) record your voice and a friend's and compare playback with the real voices (Frontiers for Young Minds, suggested experiment).
- **Rejected: "hearing yourself through a cave"** (a laryngologist's quote on Wikipedia). It suggests echo, which is not the mechanism.

## 7. Go deeper thread
Bone-conduction headphones play sound through the bones of your skull to your inner ear, which leaves your ears free to hear the room: the same inside route your own voice uses, now used on purpose.
*high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_conduction ("transmit sound to the inner ear through the bones of the skull, leaving the ears free to pick up sound from the environment")*

## Notes
- **A. Pitch direction is the contested point.** Hypothesis: the head acts as a low-pass filter, so a recording should sound higher (Royal Society 2023: "assumed", exact transfer function "not yet been formally defined"). Test: most participants thought their recording was lowered (Canadian Acoustics 2025 abstract; I opened the abstract page, not the full text, because the PDF was unreadable). Reliable middle ground: the live voice has more low-frequency energy; "fuller/thinner" is safe, "higher/lower" is not.
- **B. Microphone and speaker caveat.** The 2009 auditory.org thread frames the recording as air-only and differing from speech "recorded at ear level" (i.e. position matters). Fixed statement for the copy: others hear you only through air; a recording is also air-only; how close it is depends on microphone placement and playback.
- Open caveat on sources: auditory.org is a 2009 mailing-list post by a hearing scientist, so claims 5, 6, 10 lean on it only together with a second source; the Wikipedia pages are secondary and used for plain statements the papers also support.
