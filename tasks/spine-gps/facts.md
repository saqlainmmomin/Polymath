# Fact sheet: How does your phone find you when it has no mobile signal?

Checked 2026-10-28. Sources fetched and quoted, not recalled: archive.gps.gov (accuracy page, space segment page, GPS overview and poster), Wikipedia (Global Positioning System, GPS signals, Assisted GPS, Satellite navigation, Pseudorange, Error analysis for the Global Positioning System, Trilateration, True-range multilateration, Speed of light, Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System), ESA Navipedia (GPS Space Segment), SEOS project (trilateration page), National Weather Service (thunder page), Harvard BioNumbers (blink, via search summary). Not found or only secondary: www.gps.gov live pages returned 404 for every direct fetch (the same text was read on archive.gps.gov and in search excerpts of the live pages); the FAA how-it-works page returned 403; no primary source for "the second crossing point is far out in space" beyond two teaching sites (SEOS, university course notes via search), so that point is marked medium-high; the "airplane mode keeps GPS on" point rests on Apple forum posts only, so it is not used as a claim.

Scope: a phone's satellite fix, outdoors, with a clear view of the sky. Not in scope: Wi-Fi and mobile-tower positioning (mentioned only as myths), indoor positioning, how the map itself is drawn, the maths of solving for position.

## 1. Is the title literally true?
- **"How does your phone know exactly where you are?"** (current) Not safe. "Exactly" is false: the official figure is about 5 metres in the open and worse near buildings, bridges and trees. It is also the textbook "how X works" framing with no twist. Don't use.
- **"How does your phone find you with no mobile signal?"** True. Wikipedia: GPS "does not require the user to transmit any data, and operates independently of any telephone or Internet reception." The reader already holds the belief this challenges (a map needs signal), so it is a real twist. It is a daily-use moment (hiking, a motorway dead zone, a foreign country with no data plan). A plain question, 10 words. Two cautions the writer must respect: say "mobile signal" (phone-network bars), never just "signal", since the satellites' radio signal is itself a signal; and the title claims the position fix, not the map picture (map tiles still need data or a downloaded map). **Best.**
- **"How does your phone find you by only listening?"** True for the satellite fix (the phone transmits nothing to the satellites). Plain, and a good surprise, but the reader has no prior belief it challenges and "only" is risky: a phone also uses Wi-Fi and mobile networks for other kinds of location. Medium.
- **"How can your phone find you when satellites don't know it exists?"** True (a receiver sends nothing, so no satellite can know a phone is there). But "know it exists" is faintly personified, and the premise (satellites might know) is one few readers hold. Medium to weak.
- **"Why does your phone need a fourth satellite to tell where you are?"** True (four are needed to solve for three coordinates plus the phone's own clock error). But it hands the reader a number they have no question about, and a "need a fourth" answer arrives late in the story. Weak.
- **"How does your phone know where you are without ever calling a satellite?"** True but "calling" is figurative and invites "so how does it hear them", the same twist with a looser word. Medium.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
Each satellite keeps broadcasting the time it sent its signal, and your phone, which sends nothing back, measures how late each one arrives; that delay is a distance, and distances from several satellites leave only one spot you can be standing on.

## 3. Safe claims
1. Satellites circle about 20,200 km above Earth, and each one goes round twice a day.
   *high — archive.gps.gov/systems/gps/space ("GPS satellites fly in medium Earth orbit (MEO) at an altitude of approximately 20,200 km (12,550 miles)." "Each satellite circles the Earth twice a day.")*
2. Wherever you stand on Earth, at least four of these satellites are above your horizon.
   *high — same page ("This 24-slot arrangement ensures users can view at least four satellites from virtually any point on the planet."). "Virtually" is the source's hedge; "wherever you stand with a clear sky" is the plain form, and "at least four" holds for the whole planet, so the line is safe if the writer says "with a clear view of the sky".*
3. Every satellite carries a clock far more exact than any watch.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System (atomic clocks on board the satellites synchronised "to within a few nanoseconds"); archive.gps.gov/systems/gps/space ("Advanced atomic clocks" on the newest satellites). Don't print "atomic" or "nanosecond"; the plain sentence needs neither.*
4. Each satellite keeps sending out a radio signal that says where the satellite is and what time it sent the signal.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS_signals (the navigation message conveys "The GPS date and time, and the satellite's status" and "The ephemeris: precise orbital information for the transmitting satellite"). Write "time it was sent", never "the time now": by the time it arrives, it is already out of date.*
5. Your phone only receives this signal; it sends nothing to the satellites.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System ("It does not require the user to transmit any data").*
6. So the satellite fix needs no mobile signal and no internet.
   *high — same page ("...and operates independently of any telephone or Internet reception"). Safe because it says "the satellite fix"; do not say the phone never uses the network for anything.*
7. A radio signal travels at the speed of light, about 300,000 km every second, so one from straight overhead reaches you in about 0.07 seconds.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light ("299792458 m⋅s−1"; light is the speed of electromagnetic waves, and radio is one); 20,200 km ÷ 299,792 km/s = 0.067 s. Say "straight overhead"; a satellite near the horizon is farther and takes longer.*
8. Your phone compares the time the signal says it was sent with the time it arrived, and the gap times the speed of light is the distance.
   *high — archive.gps.gov/multimedia/poster ("distance equals the speed of light multiplied by the travel time", read via fetch summary, not verbatim).*
9. One distance on its own leaves a whole ring of places on the ground where you could be, and a second satellite's ring crosses the first in two spots.
   *high — geometry (a sphere meets the ground in a circle; two circles meet in at most two points); same idea at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trilateration ("narrow the possible locations down to no more than two" for three spheres). This is the flat-map picture of the real three-dimensional problem; it is a fair picture because you are on the ground.*
10. Three satellites leave two possible spots, and only one of them is on Earth; the other is far out in space.
    *medium-high — SEOS project trilateration page ("Only one of these positions is located on Earth, so that the other position may be disregarded altogether."); a second teaching source (search excerpt): "The other is normally far from the earth's surface". The source says "normally"; the writer's plain sentence drops it because the second spot is the first one's mirror image across the plane of the three satellites, and for real satellites 20,000 km up that is far out in space. Keep as "far out in space", never "usually".*
11. Your phone's own clock is nowhere near as exact as a satellite's, and a clock error of one millionth of a second is a distance error of about 300 metres.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorange ("A clock with an accuracy of one in a million will introduce an error of one millionth of a second each second. This error multiplied by the speed of light gives an error of 300 meters." "Typically a quartz oscillator is used in the receiver to do the timing. The accuracy of quartz clocks in general is worse ... than one in a million."). Arithmetic: a thousandth of a second is 300 km.*
12. So the phone listens to a fourth satellite, and that extra distance lets it work out how wrong its own clock is and fix it.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System ("at a minimum, four satellites must be in view of the receiver for it to compute four unknown quantities (three position coordinates and the deviation of its own clock from satellite time)"); Pseudorange page ("By finding the pseudo-range of an additional fourth satellite ... the time error can also be estimated").*

Spare, if the owner wants them:
13. In the open, a phone can place you within about 5 metres; it does worse next to buildings, bridges and trees.
    *high — archive.gps.gov/systems/gps/performance/accuracy ("GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within a 4.9 m (16 ft.) radius under open sky" and "their accuracy worsens near buildings, bridges, and trees"). Written as "can place you within about 5 metres", which is true without "typically" or "usually".*
14. The United States runs GPS, but Russia, China and the European Union run their own satellite systems too, and a phone can listen to all of them at once.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation ("four operational GNSS systems": GPS, GLONASS, BeiDou, Galileo; a smartphone caption shows "simultaneous reception and use of GNSS satellites (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou)"). India's own system, NavIC, covers India and about 1,500 km around it, with phones supporting it "since the first half of 2020" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Regional_Navigation_Satellite_System).*
15. Counting seconds between a lightning flash and its thunder gives the storm's distance: about 3 seconds for every kilometre.
    *high — https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder ("divide by 5, you'll get the distance in miles"; "5 seconds = 1 mile"); 3 s per km is the same speed of sound (about 340 m/s) in metric. Needed only if the thunder analogy is used.*

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"Your phone sends a message up to the satellites" or "the satellites track you."** The satellites broadcast to everyone and never receive anything from you (claim 5). Don't say satellites "find", "see" or "know" you. Don't claim your phone sends nothing at all: apps and the phone's other location tools do talk to networks, separately.
- **"It needs mobile signal, data or Wi-Fi."** The satellite fix needs none (claim 6). But the network can speed up the first fix: without help a receiver "may need as long as 12.5 minutes" to download satellite positions from the slow satellite link (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assisted_GPS). Don't say "instant" or "always works"; say what the fix needs, not what it never uses.
- **"The blue dot is GPS" / "GPS is the map."** The satellites give only your position; the map is drawn separately and needs data or a downloaded map. The title must not promise a working map.
- **"Three satellites are all it needs."** Three are enough only if the phone's clock were perfect; a real phone also needs the fourth (claims 11 and 12). A slide may stop at three if it says the clock is solved next, not if it says "three is enough".
- **"GPS triangulates."** It uses distances, not angles; angles are triangulation (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True-range_multilateration). Never use "triangulate". "Trilateration" is a technical word with no use later in the topic; leave it out.
- **"A satellite sits above you / fixed over a spot."** GPS satellites circle twice a day (claim 1); they do not hover.
- **"The signal is instant."** It takes about 0.07 seconds from straight overhead; the whole method depends on that delay being measurable.
- **"The phone's clock matches the satellite's."** It does not (claim 11). The thunder trick works because a person starts counting at the flash; a phone has no flash.
- **"About 30 GPS satellites" as what the phone uses.** There are 31 operational GPS satellites ("As of July 3, 2023", archive.gps.gov), but a phone can also listen to Russian, Chinese and European satellites (claim 14), so a count of "30" understates what it hears and drops out if the word "GPS" does. Leave the count out.
- **"Anywhere on Earth, a few metres."** Not indoors, underground, under thick cover or between tall buildings, and only about 5 metres in the open (claim 13).
- **"Satellite clock and signal speed are the same story as thunder."** See section 6.
- **Relativity** and "satellite clocks run fast" as a side comment. True (claim in section 7), but not part of the main answer; don't let it leak in half-explained.

## 5. One good number
The signal from a satellite straight overhead takes about 0.07 seconds to reach you. Everyday comparison: that is shorter than a blink (a blink lasts between 0.1 and 0.4 seconds), yet it is a trip of 20,200 km, and a phone can still measure it.
*Source: 20,200 km (archive.gps.gov space page) ÷ 299,792 km/s (Wikipedia, speed of light) = 0.067 s; blink 100 to 400 ms (Harvard BioNumbers "Average duration of a single eye blink", read as a search excerpt: medium on the comparison, high on the arithmetic). Say "about 0.07 seconds" and "straight overhead", never a more exact figure. Alternative if a slide is about the clock: "an error of one millionth of a second is 300 metres" (claim 11, high). Use only one of the two in the whole topic, and don't print "20,200 km" next to it (the old picture did; the text then has to explain three numbers).*

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **Counting seconds from lightning to thunder.** Right for "a delay can be turned into a distance" (3 seconds for every kilometre). Misleads in two ways. First, the reader's obvious next thought is "so what is the flash?": nothing in the satellite story plays that part, since the satellite's message of when it sent the signal is the flash, and the phone's own clock is the stopwatch, and a stopwatch started by a flash is always perfectly in step while the phone's clock is not. That hides the whole reason for the fourth satellite. Second, it can be heard as "a satellite signal is like a loud sound", but it is a radio signal about 900,000 times faster than sound. **Use as the one analogy, only for the idea that a delay is a distance, and say the satellite signal is far faster than sound; don't call the signal "a very fast thunderclap", and never say the phone and satellite clocks agree.**
- **A friend says "I'm 1 kilometre from the school."** Right for "one distance leaves a whole ring". It stays right on the obvious next thought (another landmark gives another ring). Misleads only because it is a second analogy, and the old topic ran it alongside the storm. It is also just the flat-map picture itself: a ring around a point is the real shape on the ground, not a stand-in for something else. **Don't write it as a story. Let the ring picture show it.**
- **A radio station you can hear but cannot talk to.** Right for "the phone only listens, the satellite never knows". Misleads if the reader pictures one station whose hearing depends on a tuner they own: the thing that matters is that the signal carries the time. Also a second analogy. **Not needed.**
- **Giving a taxi driver three landmarks.** Misleads: landmarks give directions, not delays; the reader hears "triangle", which is exactly the angle idea GPS does not use. **Avoid.**

## 7. Go deeper thread
The satellites' clocks are set to tick a little slower before launch. Up there, far from Earth's pull and moving fast, they would otherwise run about 38 millionths of a second ahead of clocks on the ground every day, and that would pile up into a position error of about 11 kilometres a day. Engineers fixed this before launch by changing how fast the clocks tick, one of the few places where Einstein's ideas about time are used by something in your pocket.
*38.6 microseconds a day and about 11.4 km a day: high, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_analysis_for_the_Global_Positioning_System ("Combined, these sources of time dilation cause the clocks on the satellites to gain 38.6 microseconds per day relative to the clocks on the ground." "Without correction, errors of roughly 11.4 km/day would accumulate in the position."); the clock rate is adjusted from 10.23 MHz to 10.22999999543 MHz, same page. Write "about 38 millionths of a second" and "about 11 kilometres", rounded; not on the main path, so keep it out of the slides unless the owner wants a final "more" card.*

## 9. Replacement words
- **"GPS".** It stands for Global Positioning System, named for the American system run by the United States Space Force. Both "global" and "positioning" are plain words, but a reader needs none of it, and "GPS satellites" is no longer even the full story, since phones also listen to Russian, Chinese and European satellites. **Avoid the acronym in the copy.** Say "satellites" (or "navigation satellites" the first time if a word is needed). If the owner insists on one mention, write "the American system called GPS" once, as a name, not a definition; but the rules say no acronyms, so leave it out.
- **"Usually".** There is nothing for it to hedge once the sentence is rewritten: claim 10 ("Only one of the two spots is on Earth. The other is far out in space.") and claim 13 ("can place you within about 5 metres"). Never "usually", "typically", "normally" or "mostly" in the copy; if a claim needs one, rewrite the claim.
- **"Signal".** Two meanings in one topic (mobile bars and the satellites' radio message). Use "mobile signal" only in the title and the first slide, then "radio signal" or "message" for the satellites; one word for one thing.
- **"Atomic clock", "nanosecond", "orbit", "trilateration", "ephemeris".** Not needed. "A clock far more exact than any watch", "millionth of a second", "circle Earth", nothing.
- **"Pseudorange".** Never; it is the technical name for "a distance that includes the phone's clock error".
