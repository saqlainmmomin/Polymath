# memory-consolidation (owner approved 2026-11-09)

**Title:** How does a new memory become one that lasts?
**Setup:** The second of three memory steps, told on its own: what happens to a memory after it's made.

**Spoken paragraph:**
[1] A memory is a set of brain cells that fired together during an experience, now linked so they can fire together again. But a brand-new memory is like wet cement: it's soft at first, and it needs time to set. [2] Turning it into a memory that lasts is called consolidation. Within hours, those cells build new proteins, the building material that makes their links sturdier. [3] A memory also has pieces, what you saw, heard and felt, each kept in a different part of your brain. The hippocampus, a seahorse-shaped part deep in your brain, ties those pieces together into one memory. [4] In deep sleep, the hippocampus replays your day to the cortex, the brain's outer layer, gradually moving it into long-term storage. [5] That's why students who sleep after studying remember more than students who stay up all night. Learning doesn't end when you close the book, so an all-nighter works against you.
