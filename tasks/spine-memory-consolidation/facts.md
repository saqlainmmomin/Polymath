# Fact sheet: How does a new memory become one that lasts?

## 1. Is the title literally true?
Yes. Brain scientists call this process consolidation: a memory that has just formed is easy to lose, and over the following hours (and, for some memories, much longer) the brain makes it stable. The title must not promise that every memory lasts; most new memories are forgotten, and consolidation is how the ones that stay are made to stay. Because this topic stands alone, the copy should say in one line what a memory is in the brain: a pattern of brain cells that were active together during an experience, linked strongly enough that the pattern can come back later.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
A new memory starts as a fragile set of links between brain cells; in the first hours those cells build new proteins that make the links physically sturdier, and over the following nights, mostly during deep sleep, the hippocampus replays the experience so that the outer layer of the brain (the cortex) gradually comes to hold the memory more and more by itself.

## 3. Safe claims
1. A memory is stored as a pattern of connections between brain cells: the cells that were active together during the experience become more strongly linked, so the pattern can switch back on later.
   *high — https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaw4325 (Josselyn & Tonegawa 2020, "Memory engrams", Science); https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1067020 (Kandel 2001, Science)*
2. A memory that has only just formed is fragile: for a while after learning, a knock to the head, certain drugs, or learning something similar straight away can wipe it out, while the same thing done later does not.
   *high — https://itb.biologie.hu-berlin.de/~kempter/Hippocampus_Journal_Club/Articles/frankland05.pdf (Frankland & Bontempi 2005, Nature Reviews Neuroscience, Box 1)*
3. The first stage of making a memory last happens within a few hours of learning: brain cells switch on genes and build new proteins, which they use to grow new connections and strengthen old ones.
   *high — Frankland & Bontempi 2005 (above: "Synaptic consolidation is complete within hours of training"); https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1067020 (Kandel 2001)*
4. Memories that last only minutes do not need new proteins, but memories that last days or longer do: in animals, blocking protein-building right after learning stops the long-term memory from forming.
   *high — https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1067020 (Kandel 2001); Frankland & Bontempi 2005 (protein synthesis blockers block memory formation)*
5. When two brain cells are active at the same time again and again, the link between them gets stronger. (Scientists sum this up as "cells that fire together wire together".)
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebbian_theory (Hebb 1949; slogan from Carla Shatz, 1992); Kandel 2001 (above)*
6. The hippocampus is a curved part deep inside the brain, one on each side, named after the Greek word for seahorse because of its shape.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocampus*
7. The pieces of an experience (what you saw, heard and felt) are held in different parts of the cortex, and the hippocampus ties them together like an index, so that one piece, such as a smell, can bring the whole memory back.
   *high — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/hipo.20350 (Teyler & Rudy 2007, "The hippocampal indexing theory and episodic memory", Hippocampus)*
8. Without the hippocampus, people cannot make new lasting memories of events. The famous case is Henry Molaison, known as patient H.M.: in 1953 surgeons removed the inner parts of both his temporal lobes, including most of each hippocampus, to stop his seizures, and for the rest of his life he could not remember new events for more than a short while.
   *high — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2649674/ (Squire 2009, "The legacy of patient H.M. for neuroscience", Neuron); Frankland & Bontempi 2005*
9. H.M. kept his memories from childhood but lost many from the years just before his operation (about 11 years back), which tells scientists that older memories had become less dependent on the hippocampus.
   *high — Frankland & Bontempi 2005 (above: "H.M.'s retrograde amnesia extends back approximately 11 years")*
10. During deep sleep, the hippocampus replays the activity of the day: in rats, the same brain cells that fired together while the rat explored a place fire together again while it sleeps afterwards.
    *high — https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.8036517 (Wilson & McNaughton 1994, Science); https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2762 (Diekelmann & Born 2010, Nature Reviews Neuroscience)*
11. This replay during deep sleep helps make memories of facts and events last: in people, deep sleep in the first part of the night is the stage most linked to keeping them.
    *high — https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2762 (Diekelmann & Born 2010); https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/physrev.00032.2012 (Rasch & Born 2013, Physiological Reviews)*
12. Over weeks to years, the cortex comes to hold a memory more and more on its own, so the general facts of an old memory can be recalled even if the hippocampus is later damaged.
    *high — Frankland & Bontempi 2005 (gradients "from several months to several years (or even decades)"; Box 3: remote semantic memories retrievable without a hippocampus)*
13. Sleeping soon after learning helps you remember: high-school students who slept within a few hours of learning new vocabulary remembered it better than students who stayed awake, and a night without sleep after learning still hurt recall after they had caught up on sleep.
    *high — https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/13/3/259 (Gais, Lucas & Born 2006, "Sleep after learning aids memory recall", Learning & Memory; PMID 16741280)*

Notes for the writer:
- Claims 3 and 12 are two different timescales of the same process. Hours: cells build proteins and the links get sturdier. Weeks to years: the cortex gradually takes over. Say "hours" for the cell part and "for some memories, years" for the brain-wide part; do not say proteins take years or that sleep moves a memory in one night.
- Do not say the hippocampus "moves" or "transfers" a memory to the cortex like a file. What is simply true: the cortex gradually comes to hold the memory more by itself, and the hippocampus is needed less, at least for the gist (claim 12). Scientists still argue whether vivid, detailed memories of your own life ever stop needing the hippocampus (see myths). "Hands over", "the cortex takes over", or "the cortex learns it too" are safe; "copies it across and deletes it" is not.
- Claim 8: say "most of each hippocampus", not "his hippocampus was removed". A 2013 study of his brain found that about half of the hippocampal tissue had been left behind, though it was cut off from its inputs. He could still learn new skills (mirror drawing) without remembering learning them; that is a nice surprise but optional.
- Claim 1 is the "what a memory is" line the topic needs to stand alone. Keep it short.
- Claim 10 is from rats. For people, say "the brain replays" (claim 11), or name the rats.
- "Cells that fire together wire together" is a slogan, not a law; using it as a quote is fine.

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"In REM sleep the brain links new memories with older ones."** Fix: this is an idea some scientists hold, not a settled fact. Reviews disagree about whether REM sleep, deep sleep, or both do the linking. Either drop it or say "scientists think dreaming sleep may help fit new memories in with old ones". Do not give REM a job as plainly as deep sleep. Sources: Diekelmann & Born 2010; https://www.frontiersin.org/research-topics/63905/recent-advances-in-the-stabilization-and-integration-of-associative-memories
- **"The hippocampus moves memories to the cortex and then lets go."** Too strong (see the writer's note). Fix: "over time, the cortex comes to hold the memory more and more by itself."
- **"Once a memory has set, it is fixed forever."** False. Each time you recall a memory it can become changeable again for a while and has to be stabilised again (reconsolidation; Nader, Schafe & LeDoux 2000, https://www.nature.com/articles/35021052). Memories also fade and change. Do not say a consolidated memory "can never change".
- **"Each repeated activation builds proteins over hours."** Mixed up as written. Fix: repeating something strengthens the links; separately, in the hours after learning, cells build new proteins that make the strengthening last (claims 3–5).
- **"Students who sleep after studying beat those who study the same and stay up, even with equal study time."** Fix the framing: in these studies everyone studies the same material for the same time; what differs is whether sleep comes soon after. Use the study in section 5 or claim 13, not the vague version.
- **"You learn while you sleep (play a tape at night and wake up knowing it)."** False. Sleep strengthens what you learned while awake; it does not teach you new facts from scratch.
- **"An all-nighter just makes you tired the next day."** Fix: skipping sleep after studying weakens what you learned, and catching up on sleep the next night does not fully make up for it (claim 13). (Missing sleep before studying also makes learning harder, but that is the encoding step; keep it out of this topic or keep it to one clause with no number.)
- **"We only remember things in the hippocampus" / "memories live in one spot."** False. The pieces are spread across the cortex (claim 7).

## 5. One good number
In a Harvard study, students learned 40 word pairs and were tested 24 hours later, so every group had the same time and one night of sleep. Students who learned in the evening and went to sleep soon after had lost almost nothing (about 1% better than when they finished learning); students who learned in the morning and stayed awake all day before sleeping had lost about 6.5%. That is roughly 2 or 3 of the 40 pairs, gone just because sleep came later.
*Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0033079 (Payne et al. 2012, PLoS One, 24-hour retest: "0.7±2.1% vs. −6.5±2.6%", 33 vs 34 students). Solid, peer-reviewed, open. Caveats for the writer: the effect is real but modest; do not inflate it to "twice as much" or "remember far more". "2 or 3 pairs" assumes the percentages are of all 40 pairs; say "about 6 or 7 percent" if in doubt. The difference came mostly from unrelated pairs (like "dog–window"); pairs that already made sense together were kept equally well.*

Rejected: the 12-hour comparison in the same paper (+6.6% after sleep vs +0.8% after a day awake). It compares night with day, so time of day is mixed in; the 24-hour comparison is cleaner. Gais et al. 2006 (claim 13) is also good but its exact figures could not be checked here; use it without a number.

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **Wet cement (the owner's hook).** Right intuition for claims 2–3: a new memory is soft at first and hardens over hours, and a knock while it is soft can spoil it. Misleads in two ways: cement hardens on its own just by waiting, whereas memory needs work (new proteins, and sleep), and set cement is permanent, whereas memories can be reopened and changed when recalled. "Handle it wrong and it never sets" is fine for a hook. Usable for the first hours only; do not stretch it to explain sleep or the cortex, and do not say "and then it's set for life".
- **A library index card (the hippocampus as index).** The hippocampus is like a card that says where every piece of the memory is kept on different shelves, so one clue brings the whole thing back. Right intuition for claim 7 and backed by the theory's own name. Misleads only if it suggests the card is thrown away once the books are shelved (see myths). Usable.
- **Saving a file from RAM to the hard drive.** Rejected: it suggests a one-time copy and delete, in one place, overnight. Consolidation is gradual, the memory is spread out, and the old version is not cleanly erased.
