# Fact sheet: Why isn't the shop paid the moment you tap your card?

Checked 2026-10-28. Sources fetched and quoted, not recalled: Wikipedia (Authorization hold, Credit card, Debit card, Interchange fee, Contactless payment, EMV, RuPay, Interbank network, Complete graph, Payment processor, Merchant account, Unified Payments Interface, Faster Payments Service), Stripe (card authorization and settlement guides, payout timing docs, place-a-hold docs, processor vs acquirer), Adyen payments lifecycle docs, Apple Pay security page, CFPB regulatory text (found by search; direct CFPB explainer pages would not load), RBI circular on card-on-file tokenisation (via search summary). Not found: Visa or Mastercard's own pages on timing (search only returned secondary summaries), Wikipedia's Four-corner model page (404), a UK Finance or Bank of England page on card settlement timing. Where only a secondary source backs a point, it is marked medium.

Scope: a tap at a shop with a Visa or Mastercard debit or credit card (or a phone holding one). Not in scope: UPI, bank transfers and other instant payment systems (see myths), cash machines, tap-to-ride gates.

## 1. Is the title literally true?
- **"How does a card tap move your money to a shop?"** (current) Not safe as a question: it assumes the tap moves money, and the topic's own answer is "not yet". It is also the textbook "how X works" framing the owner passes over. Don't use.
- **"Why isn't the shop paid the moment you tap your card?"** (10 words) Literally true. In every card setup the sources describe, the tap asks for approval and the money moves afterwards in a separate step: Adyen ("Authorization ... reserves funds ... Capture ... transferred"), Stripe ("the funds won't be transferred right away", "the acquirer has some flexibility about when they ask to receive the funds"), Wikipedia Credit card (batches sent at end of business day, then the acquirer pays the merchant). Some shop providers pay out faster than others (Stripe lists United States 2 business days, United Kingdom 3, India 2 for domestic charges; another source says same day, next day or two days depending on provider), but none of that is "the moment you tap", so the title survives. It is a twist (the beep feels like paying), it is daily-use (everyone has tapped), and the answer is the whole topic. "Your card" covers a phone wallet too. **Best.**
- **"Why does a tap take two seconds when the shop's money takes days?"** True in spirit and it pairs the two numbers, but it states "two seconds" as fact (see claim 5: sources say "a few seconds", "usually two to three", "well under two"; no primary source gives two exactly) and "days" as fact (not for same-day payers). Needs both numbers softened, which breaks a title. Medium; fine as slide material.
- **"Why does your bank say yes in a couple of seconds but pay the shop days later?"** True with "a couple" and "days later" (Stripe: "usually within a few seconds"; shop paid "usually within a few days"). Longer (15 words, over the 12-word limit). Fallback only if "isn't paid the moment" is thought too negative.
- **"Why does your balance drop before the shop has been paid?"** Not safe as a flat statement. What drops at once is what you can spend (the "available balance", Wikipedia Authorization hold: "reducing the balance of available funds until the merchant clears the transaction"; Stripe: "the issuer will immediately reduce the balance or available credit"). What the app shows varies: Wikipedia Authorization hold says with a debit card "the actual bank balance remains unchanged during a hold, only the available balance decreases"; Wikipedia Debit card says an online (PIN) debit shows "immediately" while an offline one "merely places an authorization hold ... usually a few days later" posts; CFPB text says the "ledger balance" is "not reduced by any holds from pending transactions". Some apps show the balance falling, some show it only when posted. The true plain form is "what you can spend drops", which is a claim (6), not a title.
- **"Why does a card payment show 'pending'?"** Not safe. "Pending" is a bank's own label, used by many (CFPB: "authorized but not yet settled") but not all, and some apps show a tap as complete at once; Stripe's own hold page warns "Card statements from some issuers ... don't always distinguish between authorisations and captured (settled) payments". A reader whose app does not say "pending" holds no such premise (rule 9). Don't use as the title; "pending" can appear in a slide as "many banks mark it pending".
- **"What does the beep actually mean?"** Not safe. I found no source on what the beep means. Terminals vary: the beep can mean the card was read, not that the bank said yes. A true answer cannot be sourced here. Don't use.
- **"Why does tapping your card not pay the shop yet?"** True, but it states the twist as the question, so slide 1's surprise is spoiled. Weaker than the best.
- **Best:** "Why isn't the shop paid the moment you tap your card?" Literal truth rests on claims 7 and 9.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
Your tap only asks your bank "can this card pay this much?", and your bank's yes (which sets that amount aside so you cannot spend it twice) comes back in a couple of seconds, but the money itself is moved later, after the shop's bank has gathered many payments together, a few working days after the tap.

## 3. Safe claims
1. When you tap, the card reader sends out a short request asking whether your card can pay that amount.
   *high — https://stripe.com/resources/more/credit-card-payment-authorization-and-transaction-settlement-process (authorization sequence: card valid, sufficient funds or credit); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card (merchant submits the transaction to the acquirer, which "verifies the credit card number, the transaction type, and the amount with the issuer"). Say "a short request" or "a short message"; "request" is also fine. A tap on a rare offline-approved low-value payment skips this step (see myths), so the copy must be about taps at a shop, not "every tap".*
2. With a Visa or Mastercard card, the request reaches the shop's bank, which passes it to a card network, which passes it to your bank, and the answer comes back the same way.
   *high — https://www.takepayments.com/blog/online-and-tech/what-are-card-schemes-and-how-do-they-work/ ("Your bank forwards the request to the card scheme ... the card scheme directs it to your customer's bank"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card ("the acquirer sends the batch transactions through the credit card association"). "Reaches" is deliberate: a payments company such as Stripe or Adyen often sits between the reader and the shop's bank and relays the request (https://stripe.com/resources/more/payment-processor-vs-merchant-acquirer: processor "forwards information to the acquirer"), and a company can be both (Stripe says it "serves as both a payment processor and an acquirer"). The four places in the picture are right; "the shop's bank" is the plain name for the acquirer. Nothing in the copy needs to mention the middle company.*
3. A card network is a company, such as Visa or Mastercard, that links banks so that they can pass payment requests to each other.
   *high — https://www.takepayments.com/blog/online-and-tech/what-are-card-schemes-and-how-do-they-work/ ("act as an intermediary, enabling communication between issuing and acquiring banks during transactions"); https://www.marqeta.com/demystifying-cards-guide/card-payments-ecosystem (the network "connects these parties"). "Such as Visa" is safe because Visa and Mastercard really are this kind of company; the wording never claims every card has one in the middle (see myths for American Express and domestic cards).*
4. Your bank checks that the card is real and that you have enough money or credit to pay.
   *high — https://stripe.com/resources/more/credit-card-payment-authorization-and-transaction-settlement-process ("The card itself is valid ... There are sufficient funds or credit available to cover the amount"); https://stripe.com/resources/more/card-authorization-explained ("whether the card itself is valid, and whether there are sufficient funds or credit"). The sources list fraud checks too; two checks is fine as the plain version because they are the ones a reader can picture. Never say the checks are "only" these two.*
5. The yes or no usually comes back in a few seconds.
   *high for "a few seconds" — https://stripe.com/resources/more/credit-card-payment-authorization-and-transaction-settlement-process ("usually takes place within a few seconds"); https://stripe.com/resources/more/card-authorization-explained ("usually lasts just a few seconds"). Search summaries gave "usually within two to three seconds" and "well under 2 seconds" (secondary only). "About 2 seconds" (current copy) is a fair middle but no source I could open names exactly two: medium. Safe plain wording: "a couple of seconds" or "a few seconds". Do not write "under a second" or "instantly": the Visa claim of 0.5 seconds for a tap came from a search snippet and its PDF would not open, so it is unverified.*
6. As soon as your bank says yes, it takes that amount off what you can spend, so you cannot spend the same money twice.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorization_hold ("puts a hold of the amount approved ... reducing the balance of available funds until the merchant clears the transaction"); https://stripe.com/resources/more/card-authorization-explained ("an authorisation hold that immediately reduces the cardholder's available funds or credit by the transaction amount, preventing potential overdrafts"). "What you can spend" is deliberately neutral: with a debit card it is the money in your account that cannot be spent, with a credit card it is your credit limit that shrinks (nothing is "set aside" in an account). See section 4 for why "sets the money aside" and "the money stays in your account" are both unsafe as flat statements.*
7. At that point the money has not reached the shop; the yes is a promise to pay, and the shop collects the money later.
   *high — https://docs.adyen.com/account/payments-lifecycle (authorisation: "Funds are reserved"; capture: "reserved funds ... are transferred"); https://stripe.com/resources/more/credit-card-payment-authorization-and-transaction-settlement-process ("even though the funds won't be transferred to the business straight away"). Use "has not reached the shop", not "has not moved": with a debit card your bank may already show less money (see title section), so "the money hasn't moved" is looser than the true "the shop hasn't got it". "A promise to pay" is safe because Stripe calls an authorisation a guarantee ("Authorising a payment guarantees the amount by holding it"), but holds run out if the shop never collects (spare 14).*
8. Banks do not move money for every single tap: payments are gathered into bundles and added up, and the shop's bank is paid in totals.
   *medium-high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card ("Authorized transactions are stored in batches ... typically submitted at the end of each business day"; the acquirer's batch goes through the card association "which debits the issuers for payment and credits the acquirer"); https://docs.stripe.com/payouts ("Stripe pays out funds daily from transactions that were captured 3 working days earlier"); a search summary of Mastercard-model explainers says the network "aggregates them by issuer, and calculates the net amounts" (secondary only). Caution: modern providers often capture each payment at once (Adyen: "by default, we capture payments automatically after authorization"), and the bundling is in the money step, so say "the money moves in totals", never "the shop's machine sends one batch every night". Keep the sentence about the bank-to-bank money, not the reader.*
9. The shop is paid a few working days after your tap, not during it.
   *medium-high — https://docs.stripe.com/payouts (United States "2 business days"; United Kingdom "3 business days"; India "2 business days for domestic charges", "5 business days for international charges"); https://stripe.com/resources/more/credit-card-payment-authorization-and-transaction-settlement-process ("usually within a few days"); a search summary says settlement may be same day, next day, or two business days depending on the provider (India, secondary). See section 5 for why "1 to 3 working days" is not the safest. "A few working days" covers all of these; same-day payers pay inside it, so it stays true.*
10. Working days leave out weekends and public holidays, so a tap on a Friday evening arrives later than the same tap on a Monday.
    *high for the definition — https://docs.stripe.com/payouts ("Business days: Only includes working days, typically Monday through Friday, and excludes public holidays"; "Payouts scheduled on weekends or holidays arrive on the next business day"). The Friday example is arithmetic. Use only if a slide needs the weekend line; the audit's "Weekends don't count." is true but is a stray sentence.*
11. Many banks mark a card payment "pending" while it is waiting to become final, and the pending amount is already off what you can spend.
    *medium-high — https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/interp-11/ (CFPB text: "pending (i.e., authorized but not yet settled) debits"; "ledger balance ... not reduced by any holds from pending transactions", found by search); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorization_hold ("A pending transaction reflects the authorization hold ... a posted transaction represents actual settled funds"). "Many" is a count, not a hedge: it is the honest word because the label differs by bank (see title section). Skip the claim if the topic does not need "pending".*
12. A network in the middle means each bank needs one link to it, instead of a separate link to every other bank.
    *medium — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interbank_network (networks make "unnecessary" separate "bilateral arrangements between every pair of banks"; this is the Wikipedia page's idea for ATM networks, and the card-network case is the same hub idea); https://www.takepayments.com/blog/online-and-tech/what-are-card-schemes-and-how-do-they-work/ (the scheme is the intermediary). See section 5 for the full judgment on the numbers and the number-free version.*

Spare claims, only if a slide needs them:
13. Each tap makes a one-time code that only fits that payment, so a copied tap cannot pay a second time.
    *medium-high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMV (the chip generates an "application cryptogram" for each transaction); https://support.apple.com/en-us/101554 (the phone gives the shop's reader "a Device Account Number and a transaction-specific dynamic security code"). The second half of the sentence is the purpose these one-time codes serve; the sources say "unique for each transaction" and a search summary says a captured code "cannot be replayed" (secondary), so keep it as a plain purpose, never "can't be hacked". For a phone, Apple states "Apple doesn't store the original ... card numbers" and the shop gets a stand-in number (Device Account Number), not your card number. For a physical contactless card a secondary source says the chip does send the real card number alongside the one-time code (medium, not opened in a primary source), so "your real number is hidden" is true only for phone wallets, not a plastic card.*
14. If you tap a card and the shop never collects the money, the hold does not last for ever: holds clear after days.
    *high for "days" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorization_hold (debit holds fall off "one to eight business days" later; credit "as long as thirty days"); https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method (in-person card authorisations valid 2 days for Mastercard, Amex, Discover; Visa 5 days). Different cards and banks differ, so give no number in copy.*
15. Some cards have no separate "shop's bank" in the middle: with American Express, the card company is also the network and the card issuer.
    *high — https://www.takepayments.com/blog/online-and-tech/what-are-card-schemes-and-how-do-they-work/ ("is a payment network as well as a card issuer"); https://payrexx.com/en/glossar/card-payment-systems ("the card network is also the issuer — there is no separate acquirer"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card (American Express as both). The article-level sources are consistent but one search-based UK regulator page notes that when a three-party scheme licenses others it becomes a four-party scheme, and Amex does license some banks to issue its cards (Wikipedia, American Express), so "Amex is always three-party" is untrue. This claim is only a spare; do not put it in a slide.*
16. A few countries run their own card network between banks: in India, RuPay is run by the National Payments Corporation of India.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RuPay ("conceived and owned by the National Payments Corporation of India"; "an alternative to Visa and Mastercard" per https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debit_card). Other examples on the Debit card page: Interac (Canada), EFTPOS (Australia, New Zealand), Girocard (Germany). Spare only; the idea (a company in the middle linking banks) is the same, so the copy does not need it.*
17. The shop gets a little less than you paid, because a small fee is shared among your bank, the card network and the shop's bank.
    *medium-high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interchange_fee ("The card-issuing bank ... deducts the interchange fee from the amount it pays the acquiring bank"; the article's worked example has the retailer getting "approximately $98" of a $100 sale, an illustration, not a rule). Fees differ by country and card, so give no number. Go deeper material (section 7).*

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"The tap moves your money to the shop."** The tap only asks for a yes. The shop is paid later (claims 7 to 9). Current title carries this myth.
- **"The money moves when the bank says yes."** The yes is a promise; the money reaches the shop later. Do not use "payment goes through" for the tap.
- **"Your bank sets the money aside" / "The money stays in your account for now" (current card 4).** True only for a debit card, and only loosely there: the hold lowers what you can spend, while the account balance itself may stay unchanged until the payment posts (Wikipedia Authorization hold; CFPB ledger vs available) and many apps show the lower figure at once. With a credit card nothing sits in an account to be set aside; your credit limit shrinks (Wikipedia Credit card: the hold "reserves that amount of the cardholder's credit limit"). Debit cards that use PIN-style "online debit" can take the money from your account at once (Wikipedia Debit card: "the debits are reflected in the user's account immediately"). One word covers all of this: "what you can spend". Never write "the money stays in your account".
- **"You can spend 50, pay 20, you can spend 30" is the picture, not the bank's ledger.** Right as a picture of "available to spend". Don't label it "balance".
- **"Every tap asks your bank."** A tap approved on the spot without asking the bank is possible: EMV describes a transaction approved offline with a "Transaction certificate (TC)" ("Transactions go online when an ARQC has been requested", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMV), and tap-to-ride gates often work from a stored decision. Say "a tap at a shop", never "every tap".
- **"The shop's card reader talks to the shop."** Not what the sources say: the reader sends the request on to a payments company or the shop's bank (Stripe processor-vs-acquirer page). "The shop's bank" is the plain name; do not say "the shop's own computer".
- **"The shop sees your balance."** I found no source and the sources describe a yes-or-no answer (authorisation approval), not a balance. Don't say it either way.
- **"Visa/Mastercard is in the middle of every card payment."** Not true of American Express (three-party: network and issuer are one company, spare 15), nor of cards run on a country's own network (RuPay in India, Interac in Canada, EFTPOS, Girocard; spare 16), nor of a payment where your bank and the shop's bank are the same bank (that case is a common shortcut in practice but I found no source; do not claim it). Safe wording names the example: "a card network, a company such as Visa or Mastercard".
- **"The money moves between the banks at the moment of tap."** The bank-to-bank step happens later in totals (claim 8).
- **"All digital payments take days."** Not true: UPI is "an Indian instant payment system" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unified_Payments_Interface) and UK Faster Payments moves money "within a few seconds" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faster_Payments_Service). The delay is how card payments are built, not a law of digital money. If the shop owner shows a UPI QR, that is a different path. Do not compare in the copy unless the topic is rewritten around it.
- **"1 to 3 working days" as a rule.** See section 5.
- **"Banks add them up and settle once, all at once."** Too tidy: shops are paid on the provider's schedule (daily payouts of what was captured 2 or 3 working days ago, Stripe), so there is no single moment. Say "in totals", not "all at once".
- **"A day or two later."** Understates: Stripe's documented UK default is 3 business days, India and US 2, and weekends push it later.
- **"It takes two seconds exactly."** See claim 5.
- **"A card network saves a huge number of links" (current card 3).** See section 5. Counts are exact only for the imagined all-to-all picture.
- **"Your real card number is hidden when you tap."** True for a phone wallet (Apple: "Apple doesn't store the original ... card numbers"; the shop gets a stand-in number), not established for a plastic card (a secondary source says the physical chip sends the real number with a one-time code, medium). Say "a one-time code" (spare 13) or say nothing.
- **"Tokenisation" as used by RBI.** The RBI circular (September 2021, RBI/2021-22/96) is about card details that online shops save ("card-on-file", effective 1 January 2022, "no entity ... other than the card issuers and/or card networks, shall store the actual card data"). It is not about the tap. Don't use it to support a tap claim.
- **Fee numbers** ("$98 of $100", "the interchange fee is 1.75"). Illustrative on Wikipedia; fees differ by country; leave out.

## 5. One good number
The yes comes back in a couple of seconds; the shop's money takes a few working days. Everyday comparison: it is the difference between sending a text and posting a parcel: the text asks "is this OK?" and the answer arrives while you wait, but the parcel needs days. **Medium** as a ratio: 2 seconds against 2 days is about 86,000 times longer by arithmetic (2 days = 172,800 seconds), but the ratio depends on "about 2 seconds" (medium, claim 5) and on which provider pays out, so say "seconds against days" and do not print a ratio.
*Source: https://stripe.com/resources/more/credit-card-payment-authorization-and-transaction-settlement-process ("usually takes place within a few seconds"; "usually within a few days"); https://docs.stripe.com/payouts (2 or 3 business days).*

**Settlement timing: is "1 to 3 working days" safe?** Documented defaults (Stripe docs): United States 2 business days, United Kingdom 3 business days, India 2 business days for domestic charges and 5 for international. A search summary of Indian explainers says same day, next day or two business days depending on provider. So 1 to 3 working days fits most defaults but is not a rule: it is too tight for international charges, too wide for providers who pay same day, and "T+1" and "T+2" are provider settings, not law (Stripe: "Settlement timing varies per country and is typically expressed as 'T+X' days"). It also names the wrong thing: those are payouts to the shop's bank account, while the card network's own settlement between banks is a separate step. "A day or two later" understates the UK default of 3 and also the weekend effect. **Safest plain sentence: "The shop is usually paid a few working days after your tap."** Where the UK or India reader asks: UK default is 3 working days in Stripe's docs, India 2; I found no UK Finance, Bank of England, RBI or NPCI page that sets a card payout time. Do not put T+2 or any "T+" term in copy; it is not needed.

**Is the link counting (45 against 10) a safe simplification?** Maths is exact: all-to-all among n banks needs n(n-1)/2 links (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_graph: "Kn has n(n - 1)/2 edges"), so 10 banks need 45; one hub needs n, so 10. It is a true simplification of the idea, because a shared network lets a bank link up once and reach every other bank on it (Wikipedia Interbank network). It misleads in four ways: (a) real banks are not all linked to each other in any case, so "45" was never a real count; (b) real banks join more than one network, often through a processor, so "one link" is the idea, not the count; (c) some payments do not need the network (same company on both sides, see myths); (d) the current picture draws shops' banks against customers' banks, 3 x 3 = 9 lines against 6, while the text says 45 against 10, so the arithmetic in the picture does not match the words. Judgment: the idea is safe, the number is a made-up example and must be labelled "picture 10 banks"; I would drop the number. **Number-free version: "A network in the middle means a bank joins once and can then reach every other bank on it."** If the owner keeps the number, the safe wording is "10 banks, all joined to each other, would need 45 links; joined through one network, 10." Do not say "thousands of banks save a huge number of links": the bank count is unsourced here.

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **A "reserved" sticker on a shop item.** Right for the hold: the item is set aside so nobody else can take it, but it has not been sold yet. Misleads if the reader pictures a sticker on the shop's goods rather than on their own spendable money, and if it suggests the shop is in charge of the hold; your bank is. Also the sticker never expires, while a hold does (spare 14). **Use only for "set aside, not yet sold", and say the bank puts it on your spendable money.**
- **A promise to pay (an IOU) now, cash later.** Right for the whole topic: the shop gets your bank's yes at once and the money later. Misleads if the reader hears "a promise can be broken": card approvals are called guarantees by Stripe, though holds run out if the shop never collects. Also a paper IOU suggests a person signs; here it is a computer. **Use; say "your bank's promise".**
- **An airport hub: fly to the hub, then onward, rather than a direct flight between every pair of cities.** Right picture for why a network sits in the middle (one link each, not one to every other). Misleads if the reader thinks every flight must change at the hub (some routes are direct, spare 15 and 16 show other set-ups) and, with a number attached, if the reader hears a real count. **Use for the network slide in one line, number-free.**
- **A text message against a parcel (for the number).** Right for "answer in seconds, money in days". Misleads if the reader thinks the money travels as a parcel by post; it moves between bank accounts in totals. **Use only as a one-line comparison on the number, if at all.**
- **A restaurant table booked with a card.** Misleads: a booking reserves a table, not money, so it does not show the hold. A hotel hold is better (Stripe: "hotels often authorise a payment in full before a guest arrives, then capture the money when the guest checks out"). That is a real instance of the same thing, not an analogy. **Don't use as a comparison; use the hotel as an example with spare 14 if a slide needs one.**
- **Cheque.** Misleads: a cheque is a payment on paper that can bounce and be cleared days later; a tap's yes is checked first. **Don't use.**

## 7. Go deeper thread
When you tap, the shop gets a little less than you paid: your bank, the card network and the shop's bank each take a small fee out of the amount as the money moves back along the same four places, and the money that finally reaches the shop is what is left. The fee that goes to your bank is called the interchange fee.
*Fee taken from the amount the issuing bank pays the acquiring bank: high, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interchange_fee ("The card-issuing bank in a payment transaction deducts the interchange fee from the amount it pays the acquiring bank"); that the network and the shop's bank also take a charge: medium-high, same page (worked example only). Fee limits by country were not returned by the page I read, so leave them out. Give no percentage or dollar amount: the $98 of $100 example is illustrative. Alternative thread: a hold that outlives the tap, holds at hotels and fuel pumps (https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method; hold lengths differ by card and bank, so no number).*
