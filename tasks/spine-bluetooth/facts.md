# Fact sheet: How does Bluetooth keep working in a room full of Wi-Fi?

Checked 2026-11-05 by a separate fact-checker subagent (17 claims, primary sources fetched) plus my own fetches. Primary sources: Bluetooth Core Specification v5.4 HTML (Baseband, Radio, LE Link Layer) at bluetooth.com/wp-content/uploads/Files/Specification/HTML/Core-54/out/en/ (cited below as BASEBAND, RADIO, LL); Bluetooth SIG Tech Overview (https://www.bluetooth.com/learn-about-bluetooth/tech-overview/), SIG Range page, SIG Origin page; 47 CFR 15.247 (eCFR). Secondary: Wikipedia (Bluetooth, ISM radio band, List of WLAN channels, Hedy Lamarr), TI app note SWRA475A, Nordic coexistence docs. Not found: a 2025-26 market-share figure for LE Audio vs Classic earbuds (see claim 4 scope); a measured transmit power for a typical earbud (only the class limit is sourced).

Scope (round 2 note: the copy no longer says "original design"; it says "dozens of channels" and "up to 1,600", both true of Bluetooth as a whole, and the exact 79 and the 40 of Low Energy stay on this sheet): Bluetooth Classic (BR/EDR), the kind that has streamed music to earbuds, speakers and cars for years, in a connected link. Not in scope: Bluetooth Low Energy details (mentioned only as a myth), LE Audio, pairing and security, range, the name's history, how audio is compressed.

## 1. Is the title literally true?
**Round 2 (approver asked for the stake in the title): "Why doesn't Wi-Fi drown out your Bluetooth earbuds?"** True: Wi-Fi sharing the band does not drown out Bluetooth links, which is the whole point of hopping; "drown out" means overpower completely, and claim 9 shows a clash can still cost a moment of music, so the title does not say "never affects". It puts the reader's earbuds in it and the reader already feels the premise (Wi-Fi is everywhere and strong, earbuds are tiny). 8 words, no acronym. Setup: "They share the same airwaves, and Wi-Fi can be far stronger." (claims 2, 3, with "can" for 5 GHz-only routers). Other titles tried: "How do your Bluetooth earbuds hear your phone over all the Wi-Fi?" (cute, "hear" figurative), "Why don't your Bluetooth earbuds and your Wi-Fi jam each other?" ("jam" overclaims), "How do your earbuds and your Wi-Fi share the same air?" (a friend would shrug: no tension). Round 1 title below, kept for the record.
- "How does Bluetooth keep working in a room full of Wi-Fi?" (round 1, replaced: no stake the reader holds) True. Bluetooth connections do keep working next to Wi-Fi; the method (hopping plus skipping bad channels) is in the spec. It claims "keep working", not "never glitch" (claim 9 says a hop can lose a piece). No brand or acronym beyond the topic's own name. 11 words. Test: a friend would not shrug, because most people have never wondered why two radios in the same room don't wreck each other. Medium now.
- "How do your earbuds keep playing in a room full of Wi-Fi?" True and more personal, but earbuds could be LE Audio (hops differently), and "Bluetooth" is missing from the feed card. Medium.
- "Why doesn't Wi-Fi drown out Bluetooth?" Weak: premise ("Wi-Fi would drown it out") is one few readers hold, and it is slightly false (coexistence is imperfect).
- "How does Bluetooth work?" (the owner's own words) True but the textbook framing with no tension. Don't use.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
Bluetooth shares its airwaves with much stronger Wi-Fi by never staying on one channel: both devices hop together through 79 narrow channels, as often as 1,600 times a second, so a clash costs one tiny piece of the music, and they skip the channels that keep failing.

## 3. Safe claims
1. Bluetooth is a short-range radio link: two devices send data to each other directly as radio waves.
   *high — SIG Tech Overview; SIG Range page ("anywhere from more than a kilometer down to less than a meter"). Don't print a range.*
2. Many Wi-Fi routers send on the same unlicensed stretch of airwaves that Bluetooth uses, 2.400 to 2.4835 GHz, along with microwave ovens and baby monitors.
   *high — RADIO Table 2.1 ("Regulatory Range 2.400 GHz to 2.4835 GHz ... f=2402+k MHz, k=0,...,78"); Wikipedia "ISM radio band" (microwave oven at 2.45 GHz; baby monitors, cordless phones); Wikipedia "Wi-Fi" (Wi-Fi 4 and 6 use 2.4 and 5 GHz, Wi-Fi 5 uses 5 GHz only). Write "many routers" / "can", never "your router does": a router on 5 GHz only does not clash. Don't print "2.4 GHz" in the copy; "the same stretch of airwaves" needs no number.*
3. A router can be far stronger than earbuds: the earbud class Bluetooth allows tops out at 2.5 milliwatts, while a router may legally send 100 milliwatts (EU) to 1 watt (US).
   *high on the limits — RADIO Table 3.1 (Class 2 up to 2.5 mW); 47 CFR 15.247 (1 W peak conducted, 2400-2483.5 MHz); EU 100 mW EIRP (ETSI EN 300 328, seen on secondary sites only: medium). Ratio 40x to 400x. Typical earbud output is not sourced (JBL sheets list only a limit), so the copy says "can be far stronger", never a measured figure. Class 1 Bluetooth (up to 100 mW) exists but is not what earbuds use.*
4. The kind of Bluetooth that has long streamed music (Classic) divides the airwaves into 79 channels, spaced 1 MHz apart.
   *high — RADIO ("RF channels are spaced 1 MHz ... f=2402+k MHz, k=0,...,78"); SIG Tech Overview ("79 channels with 1 MHz spacing"). Say "spaced", not "wide" (the spec says spacing). Scope: Classic only; LE has 40 channels. Most earbuds on sale today still stream over Classic (A2DP): qualitative only, ABI Research April 2024 calls LE Audio "still in its infancy"; no share number is used, and the copy says "Bluetooth's original design" (Classic) instead of "your earbuds use".*
5. One Wi-Fi network sits on a wide block of those channels at once: about 20 MHz, roughly a quarter of the 79.
   *medium-high — Wikipedia "List of WLAN channels" (802.11b "occupies 22 MHz"; 20 MHz nameplate for later versions; US non-overlapping 1/6/11). 20/79 is about 25%. Copy says "a wide block", no figure.*
6. While connected, a Classic device sends on one channel at a time; the channel stays fixed for the whole packet.
   *high — BASEBAND ("The RF frequency shall remain fixed for the duration of the packet.").*
7. Classic devices can change channel as often as 1,600 times a second: time is cut into slots of 625 millionths of a second, and a new channel can start each slot.
   *high, as a maximum — BASEBAND 2.1 ("The maximum hop rate is 1600 hops per second in the Connection state ... 3200 in the Inquiry and Page substates"); 2.2.3 ("each 625 us in length"). 1 / 625 us = 1,600. It is the most, not a fixed rate: a packet may span up to five slots on one channel, and power-saving modes use fewer slots. Write "as often as 1,600 times a second", never "exactly" or "always". Wikipedia's "usually 1600" is the loose version; don't copy it. A2DP rides on these same links; its actual hop count per second is lower when it uses longer packets (not sourced, so the copy says "as often as").*
8. When two devices connect, one of them (the leader) sets the order of channels and the beat; the other follows, keeping its clock in step.
   *high — BASEBAND 2.2.2 ("The frequency hopping in the piconet physical channel is determined by the Bluetooth clock and BD_ADDR of the Central. When the piconet is established, the Central's clock is communicated to the Peripherals. Each Peripheral shall add an offset to its native clock to synchronize with the Central's clock."). Mechanism: the follower gets the leader's address and clock in an FHS packet during paging (BASEBAND 6.5.1.4) and updates its offset on every packet it receives. Don't name which device leads: the phone usually does, but the spec only says whoever pages is Central, so the copy says "one sets the beat and the order".*
9. If a hop lands on a channel that is busy, only the data sent on that hop is affected. A lost packet is not acknowledged and is sent again on a later hop; for streamed music the sender gives up on a packet that is too late, and the listener hears a tiny glitch instead of a long delay.
   *high — BASEBAND 7.6 (ARQ: packets "shall be transmitted until acknowledgment of a successful reception is returned ... (or timeout is exceeded)"); 7.6.3 (flush: "retransmissions are allowed up to a certain limit at which the current payload shall be ignored"); Android/BlueZ makes the A2DP socket flushable with a timeout. The copy says "loses just one piece", which is true and not the same as "never glitches".*
10. The devices measure which channels are failing and stop using them (adaptive frequency hopping, added in Bluetooth 1.2, 2003). They must keep at least 20 of the 79 in use.
    *high — BASEBAND 2.3.1 ("shall use at least N min RF channels (where N min is 20)"); 8.6.8 (channels classified "unknown, bad or good" from "active or passive channel assessment" and failure measures). Bluetooth does not know where Wi-Fi is; it only notices its own losses (secondary: Ericsson note on AFH). Don't print the name "adaptive frequency hopping" or the 20.*
11. Bluetooth is not a weak Wi-Fi: it links two devices directly, over short range, at about 1 to 3 megabits a second, where Wi-Fi 4, 5 and 6 reach hundreds to thousands of megabits.
    *high — SIG Tech Overview (Classic BR 1 Mb/s, EDR 2 and 3 Mb/s); Wikipedia "Wi-Fi" generations table (PHY maxima, not real throughput). Not on the main path; spare.*
12. Bluetooth Low Energy, the newer low-power kind, has 40 channels and also hops, but only once per connection event, every 7.5 milliseconds to 4 seconds, so far slower than Classic.
    *high — SIG Tech Overview ("40 channels with 2 MHz spacing (3 advertising channels/37 data channels)"); LL ("connInterval ... 7.5 ms to 4.0 s"; "The same data channel index shall be used for all packets in the connection event"). Spare: this is why "1,600" and "79" must never be said of "Bluetooth" with no scope. The copy limits them with "Bluetooth's original design".*
13. (Round 2) One Wi-Fi network in the 2.4 GHz band takes up about a quarter of the stretch Bluetooth uses, for both Classic (about 20 of 79 channels) and Low Energy (about 10 of 40 channels of 2 MHz), because Wi-Fi's usual width is 20 MHz and Bluetooth's stretch is about 80 MHz.
    *medium-high — Wikipedia "List of WLAN channels" (802.11b "occupies 22 MHz"; later versions 20 MHz nameplate, about 16 MHz used); RADIO (2402 to 2480 MHz, 79 x 1 MHz); SIG Tech Overview (LE 40 x 2 MHz). 20/80 = a quarter; 22/80 = 28%. Wi-Fi can also be set to 40 MHz wide in 2.4 GHz (rare, discouraged), so the copy says "one Wi-Fi network can cover about a quarter", never "does". This is the size the reader holds the "wide block" against; the copy uses it instead of the old "79 channels" so the sentence is true of all Bluetooth and needs no "original design" scope.*
14. (Round 2) Bluetooth cuts its stretch of airwaves into dozens of narrow channels: 79 in Classic, 40 in Low Energy.
    *high — claims 4 and 12. "Dozens" is true of both kinds; the exact 79 is left out of the copy because it is Classic-only.*
15. (Round 2) A blink lasts about 0.1 to 0.4 seconds, so 1,600 hops a second is at least 160 hops in one blink.
    *arithmetic on 1,600 (claim 7, a maximum) and a blink duration from Harvard BioNumbers (read as a search excerpt in the GPS sheet: medium). The copy says "up to 1,600 times a second, over a hundred in one blink", which holds at the maximum rate. Do not write "exactly".*
16. (Round 2) What a clash costs, said plainly: each hop carries a tiny piece of the music; if it lands on busy Wi-Fi, that piece is lost and is sent again on a later hop; if it arrives too late, it is thrown away and the listener hears a short skip.
    *high — claim 9 (BASEBAND 7.6 ARQ; 7.6.3 flush; Android/BlueZ A2DP flush timeout). "a later hop", not "the next hop" or "a different channel": the spec gives the resend on a later slot, and I did not find a rule that consecutive hops differ. The skip is the sheet's own "tiny glitch".*

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"Bluetooth uses a fixed channel / one frequency."** It never sits still (claims 6, 7).
- **"Bluetooth has 79 channels" and "hops 1,600 times a second" said of all Bluetooth.** Classic only. LE has 40 channels and hops far less often (claim 12); LE Audio hops once per isochronous event (every 5 ms to 4 s, from LL; no single rate). Scope every number.
- **"It hops exactly 1,600 times a second."** It is the most, not a fixed count (claim 7). Searching for devices hops up to 3,200 a second; longer packets and power saving make the real count lower.
- **"Pairing sets the hop pattern."** Pairing is a one-time introduction where two devices swap keys so they can trust and encrypt. The hop order comes from the leader's address and clock each time they connect (claims 8; BASEBAND 2.2.2). Don't say "pair" for connecting: one word, one meaning. Pairing is not in this topic.
- **"The hop pattern is a secret code / makes Bluetooth secure."** The FHS packet carrying the address and clock is not encrypted; hopping is an interference defence, not a lock. Don't say "secret" or "only they know the order".
- **"Bluetooth avoids Wi-Fi because it knows where Wi-Fi is."** It only notices which of its own channels keep losing data (claim 10).
- **"Wi-Fi and Bluetooth never interfere."** They can, which is why the skipping exists. Chips in one phone also coordinate with a small request-and-grant signal (Nordic docs, medium). Don't claim "no interference" or "never".
- **"Bluetooth was invented by Hedy Lamarr" / "is her patent."** Her 1942 patent was an early frequency-hopping idea that was not used then; Bluetooth was designed at Ericsson in the 1990s and shares only the general idea (Wikipedia, "Similar technology was used in operational systems only beginning in 1962"). Don't mention her unless as a Go deeper with that wording.
- **"Bluetooth is a weak Wi-Fi / the same thing."** Different job (claim 11).
- **"Wi-Fi is always on the same band."** 5 GHz Wi-Fi never clashes with Bluetooth. The copy says "can use the same stretch".
- **"Park on one channel and you are under Wi-Fi".** Only a chance: about a quarter of the stretch is covered by one network, so the copy says "you could land under Wi-Fi".
- **"The router is stronger" as a flat fact.** True of the legal limits (claim 3), not of every router and earbud. Write "can be far stronger".
- **"Bluetooth range is 10 metres."** The SIG says from under a metre to over a kilometre. Leave range out.
- **Name trivia:** the SIG's page puts the suggestion at 1996, Wikipedia at 1997. Don't print a year.

## 5. One good number
**1,600.** A Classic connection can change channel as often as 1,600 times a second. Everyday comparison (arithmetic, not a source): a blink lasts 100 to 400 milliseconds, so Bluetooth could hop at least 160 times in one blink. Keep it to "as often as 1,600 times every second". The other number in the copy is 79 (channels), on a different slide. Don't print 625, 20, 2.5 or 100.

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **The stations on a radio dial.** Right for "many narrow channels side by side, and a radio is tuned to one at a time", and for both ends needing to tune together. It stays right on the obvious next thought: "then both sides have to turn the dial in step", which is the leader-and-follower claim. Misleads in one way: radio stations broadcast to everyone, whereas the hop order is just a shared pattern, not a broadcast. And it should not suggest the listener picks the channel freely. **Use as the one analogy**, and keep it to "79 narrow channels, like stations on a radio dial".
- **A crowd in a stadium (Wi-Fi) and a whisper that moves.** Medium. Right for "stronger voice". Misleads: "whisper" suggests it is quieter in general and hides that Wi-Fi covers only a block of channels. Not used.
- **Two people changing rooms.** Not needed; it would be a second analogy, and "rooms" suggests physical movement.
- **Walkie-talkies on a channel.** Misleads: walkie-talkies stay on one channel, the opposite of the point. Avoid.

## 7. Go deeper thread
**Moved here in round 2 (cut from the main story):** the devices measure which channels keep losing pieces and stop using them (claim 10, adaptive frequency hopping, at least 20 of the 79 kept). Plain line for Go deeper: "Your devices also keep score. Channels that keep losing pieces get dropped from the order, so they hop around Wi-Fi as well as through it."

Bluetooth Low Energy's three discovery channels are not part of the hopping at all: they sit at fixed spots (numbers 37, 38 and 39, at 2402, 2426 and 2480 MHz) in the gaps between the three most-used Wi-Fi channels (1, 6 and 11, centred at 2412, 2437 and 2462 MHz). Devices that are looking for each other use only those three. *Medium — TI SWRA475A states the Wi-Fi 1/6/11 gap design; the three frequencies are from electronics-notes.com and SIG Tech Overview ("3 advertising channels"). TI also measured clashes anyway. Write "sit in the gaps between", not "never clash".*
Name: Intel's Jim Kardach suggested "Bluetooth" as a temporary code name, after the 10th-century Danish king Harald Bluetooth who united Denmark; the logo joins the old runes for his initials H and B. *high — SIG Origin page.* Trivia only.

## 8. Word choices
- **channel**: one slice of the airwaves, 1 MHz apart in Classic. Define once ("79 narrow channels, like the stations on a radio dial") and never say frequency, band, 2.4 GHz or spectrum.
- **airwaves**: the shared stretch of radio. One word, not "spectrum", "air", "band". ("the same air" in the setup is allowed as everyday speech; prefer "airwaves".)
- **Wi-Fi** is the only name for the other side (round 2): not router, not Wi-Fi network, not busy Wi-Fi. "One Wi-Fi network" appears once only to size it.
- **dial**: Bluetooth's stretch of airwaves, the radio-dial picture. Say "channels" for its slices.
- **hop**: one change of channel. Introduced by action in slide 3 ("hop from channel to channel"), then "a hop". Never "jump", "switch", "flick".
- **stronger**: more transmit power. Never "louder", "shouting" (one word, one meaning).
- **the leader / the follower**: dropped in round 2; the copy says "they work out the same order of channels", which is what claim 8 amounts to for the reader. No beat, no who leads. Never "master/slave" or "Central/Peripheral", never name the phone as the leader.
- **connect**: the moment two devices join. Never "pair" in this topic.
- **piece**: what a hop carries ("Music travels in tiny pieces"). Not "packet", "data", "frame", "piece of sound".
- "Wi-Fi network", "your Wi-Fi router": the Wi-Fi side. Not "WLAN", "802.11".
- Avoid: GPS-style synonyms for the same thing, "usually", "exactly", "always", "never" unless literally true, "the Bluetooth" without the scope phrase in the one place where 79 is said.
