# Fact sheet: How does your brain decide what to remember?

## 1. Is the title literally true?
Mostly, with one fix in wording. The brain does not "decide" like a person choosing, and there is no single judge. What happens is that attention, and how much you think about something, set what gets stored and how strongly. That is a fair reading of "decide" for a curious reader, as long as the copy never says the brain makes a deliberate choice, and never promises that you can choose to remember anything you like. This topic covers encoding only, the first of three steps (encoding, consolidation, recall). It must say in one line what a memory is: a lasting change in the brain that lets you bring back something you saw, heard, did or thought. It must not explain consolidation or recall beyond naming them.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
Your senses take in far more than you could ever keep, and only what you pay attention to gets a real chance to be stored; then the more you think about what it means and link it to things you already know, the stronger the memory you make.

## 3. Safe claims
1. A memory starts with encoding: turning what you see, hear or think into a form the brain can store and bring back later. Encoding is the first of three steps; the other two are storing it for the long term (consolidation) and bringing it back (recall).
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encoding_(memory); https://pressbooks.online.ucf.edu/lumenpsychology/chapter/reading-forgetting/*
2. Your senses take in far more than you remember. Experiments that flash a grid of letters for a split second show that people briefly hold most of the grid, but the picture fades in well under a second, before they can report more than a few letters.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconic_memory (Sperling 1960); https://www.simplypsychology.org/sensory-memory.html*
3. Sounds linger a little longer than sights: an echo of what you just heard lasts a few seconds, which is why you can sometimes "replay" a question you weren't listening to.
   *high — https://www.simplypsychology.org/sensory-memory.html (echoic memory, about 2–4 seconds)*
4. Paying attention is the main gate into memory: when attention is split while something happens, people form much weaker memories of it that they can later bring back on purpose.
   *high — https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/interactions-between-attention-and-memory/ (Chun & Turk-Browne 2007: dividing attention at encoding "prevents the formation of conscious memories")*
5. Seeing something many times is not the same as noticing it. In a UCLA office building, most people who had worked there for years could not say where the nearest fire extinguisher was, but found it quickly once asked to look.
   *high — https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2016/11/Attention-and-Incidental-Memory-in-Everyday-Settings-Castel-Nazarian-Blake-MIT-Attention-Handbook-2015.pdf (Castel, Vendetti & Holyoak 2012)*
6. Things you have just noticed sit in working memory, a small mental workspace that holds only about four things at once for most adults.
   *high — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/ (Cowan 2001, "about four chunks"); https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf*
7. Working memory only keeps things while you keep them in mind. When people were kept busy so they could not repeat a short string of letters, most of it was gone after about 18 seconds.
   *high — https://www.simplypsychology.org/peterson-peterson.html (Peterson & Peterson 1959, about 80% recalled at 3 s, about 10% at 18 s)*
8. How you think about something while it is in front of you changes how well you remember it later: thinking about what a word means makes it far more memorable than noticing how it looks or sounds.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels_of_processing (Craik & Lockhart 1972; Craik & Tulving 1975)*
9. Linking a new thing to what you already know makes it easier to remember, because it gives the memory more ways to be found again.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels_of_processing (elaboration); https://www.simplypsychology.org/levelsofprocessing.html*
10. Things that stir strong feelings tend to be remembered better than dull ones, because the brain's emotion centre (the amygdala) boosts how strongly they are stored.
    *high — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2676782 ; McGaugh 2004, Annu. Rev. Neurosci. 27:1–28; Phelps 2004, Curr. Opin. Neurobiol.*
11. When people in the US were asked to draw a penny from memory, most got it badly wrong, leaving out words or putting them in the wrong place, even though they had handled pennies all their lives. Most could not pick the real penny out of a set of similar drawings either.
    *high — https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/1979-nickerson.pdf (Nickerson & Adams 1979, Cognitive Psychology 11:287–307; abstract: performance "surprisingly poor on all tasks"); https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2016/11/Attention-and-Incidental-Memory-in-Everyday-Settings-Castel-Nazarian-Blake-MIT-Attention-Handbook-2015.pdf*
12. The same happens with a logo seen everywhere: of 85 university students asked to draw the Apple logo, only one drew it correctly, and fewer than half could pick the real one out of eight look-alikes.
    *high — https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2016/11/BlakeNazarianCastel2015-AppleQJEP.pdf (Blake, Nazarian & Castel 2015, QJEP); https://www.uclahealth.org/news/85-college-students-tried-to-draw-the-apple-logo-from-memory-84-failed*
13. Forgetting where you put your keys is very often an encoding failure, not a fading memory: you put them down while thinking about something else, so the place was never properly stored. It is a normal, everyday lapse, not a sign of illness.
    *high — https://pressbooks.online.ucf.edu/lumenpsychology/chapter/reading-forgetting/ (encoding failure; absent-mindedness, Schacter's "seven sins of memory")*

Notes for the writer:
- **Hook (keys).** "Odds are you never lost that memory, you never made it" is a fair hook, but not certain for every case: sometimes you did store it and the memory got mixed up with all the other times you put your keys down. Say "often" or "most likely", not "always". Claim 13 is the safe form.
- **Penny example.** Use claim 11 (US penny) with "in the US" said once, or switch to claim 12 (Apple logo), which works for readers anywhere, including India. No study on Indian coins or notes was found. Similar results exist for British coins (Jones 1990; Jones & Martin 1992, where most people drew the Queen's head facing the wrong way). Do not use exact penny numbers like "median three of eight features": I could only find them second-hand (Pinker), not in the original paper. Lincoln faces right on the penny; if the copy asks "which way does the head face?", the answer is right. The US stopped making new pennies in 2025, so say "a penny", not "the coin in your pocket".
- **The penny result has a twist worth keeping:** with even a brief deliberate look, people later remember coin details well (Marmie & Healy 2004, https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_52550). That is exactly this topic's lesson: the details were never encoded, not "lost".
- **Working memory number.** Say "about four things". Miller's "seven, plus or minus two" (1956) is the older figure and is no longer the accepted limit; the source's "4 to 7" blends the two. Seven-digit phone numbers fit only because people group them into chunks.
- **Time limit.** Do not say "under a minute" as a fixed rule. You can hold something as long as you keep repeating it; the loss in claim 7 happens when you are distracted. Safe line: "if your mind moves on, it's gone within seconds".
- **Emotion (claim 10)** strengthens memories partly at encoding (it grabs attention) and partly during the later storing step. Keep it to "feelings make memories stronger"; don't claim it happens only at encoding. Emotional memories feel vivid but are not always accurate.
- **"Only what you pay attention to gets encoded"** is close but too absolute (see myths). Use "only what you pay attention to gets a real chance".

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"Thousands of sensory signals arrive every second."** Not sourceable as a count; there is no meaningful "number of signals". Drop it. If a number is wanted: researchers estimate each eye sends the brain roughly 10 million bits of information a second, about the speed of an old office internet connection (Koch et al. 2006, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1564115 ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/07/060726180933.htm). Caveat: this is extrapolated from guinea pig eyes, so say "scientists estimate".
- **"Working memory holds 7 items" / "4 to 7 items."** Fix: about four for most adults (Cowan 2001). Seven is the old 1956 estimate.
- **"Working memory lasts under a minute."** Fix: it lasts as long as you keep the thing in mind; once distracted, most is gone in about 20 seconds.
- **"Nothing gets in without attention."** Too strong. Some unattended things leave faint traces that change behaviour later without you being able to recall them (unconscious memory). Fix: "without attention, you almost never form a memory you can bring back".
- **"Attention works like a spotlight, and anything outside it is erased."** Fix: things outside it mostly fade within a second or a few seconds; very loud, sudden or personal things (like your own name) can pull the spotlight to them.
- **"Your brain records everything, you just can't find it."** False. Most of what reaches your senses is never stored at all. Memory is not a video recording.
- **"Repeating something over and over is the best way to remember it."** Repeating keeps it in working memory, but on its own makes a weak long-term memory; thinking about its meaning works better.
- **"Losing your keys means your memory is going bad."** Normal lapse of attention, common at every age.
- **"Most people can't draw a penny" said to a non-US reader as if it's their coin.** The study used US pennies; say so, or use the Apple logo.

## 5. One good number
Of 85 university students asked to draw the Apple logo from memory, only 1 got it right, even though most of them used Apple products every day. That is like a whole school assembly hall where just one person can draw the logo on the phone in their own pocket.
*Source: https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2016/11/BlakeNazarianCastel2015-AppleQJEP.pdf (Blake, Nazarian & Castel 2015, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology; 52 Apple users, 10 PC users, 23 both; results did not differ between them). Solid: published, small sample (85, all UCLA students), the 1-of-85 figure is in the abstract. Caveat: "correctly" meant the main features (bite, leaf) in the right places, not a perfect copy. Works worldwide, unlike the penny.*

Rejected: "thousands of signals a second" (no source). The eye's "10 million bits a second" is usable only with "scientists estimate" and is less vivid for a 10-year-old.

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **A spotlight on a dark stage.** Attention lights up a small part of what's happening, and that's what has a chance to be kept. Right intuition for the filter and for "seeing is not noticing". Misleads if read as "nothing outside the beam ever gets in", or that you can only notice one thing; loud or personal things can swing the light. Usable, with "mostly fades".
- **Velcro hooks.** A new fact with lots of links to things you already know has many hooks to catch on, so it sticks; a bare phone number has almost none. Right intuition for deep processing and linking. Misleads only if it suggests more facts crammed together always help. Usable.
- **A camera that records everything.** Rejected: this is the myth the topic corrects. The brain keeps a small, chosen part, and rebuilds the rest when you remember.
