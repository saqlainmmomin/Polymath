# Bike gears, stage 2 (paragraph for owner approval)

**Title:** Why does one gear click make the same hill feel easier?
**Setup:** Same hill, same legs.

**Paragraph:**
[1] On a steep hill you push as hard as you can and barely move. Click the gear lever once and the same hill feels easier, though your legs aren't stronger and the hill isn't flatter. So what did the click change? [2] The chain runs from the pedal ring to a stack of rings on the back wheel, and the lever slides it onto another one. Each link fits one tooth, so a ring with fewer teeth turns more times for the same chain. So a smaller back ring spins the wheel more for each pedal turn. [3] More wheel turns means each pedal turn carries you farther. A bigger back ring means a shorter way. [4] Think of walking up a steep path. Long strides are hard heaves, short quick steps are light, and you reach the same top either way. The hill is the same height in every gear too. So a shorter way per turn means a lighter push each time, and more turns to reach the top. [5] On flat road nothing pulls you back, so a smaller back ring lets each turn carry you farther, and your legs don't have to spin so fast.

**Bold line:** A gear can't make the hill any lower. It only decides how many pushes it takes to climb it.

**Go deeper:** Before chains, the pedals of the old penny-farthing were fixed straight to the hub of the huge front wheel, so there were no gears at all: one pedal turn was one wheel turn, and the only way to go farther per turn was a bigger wheel, up to about 60 inches across. The chain and a smaller back ring let a smaller wheel go as far as a big one, and that is the idea your gear lever still plays with. Even today the word "gear inches" means how wide a penny-farthing wheel would have to be to match your gear.

**Most likely to lose a reader:** slide 4, it turns "shorter way per turn" into "lighter push" in one step; the walking picture now comes first to carry it.
