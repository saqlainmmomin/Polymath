# Fact sheet: Why does one click of the gear lever make the same hill feel easier?

Checked 2026-10-28. Sources fetched and quoted, not recalled: Wikipedia (Bicycle gearing, Derailleur gears, Cadence (cycling), Bicycle performance, Mechanical advantage, Crankset, Cassette (bicycle part), Penny-farthing), plus two beginner how-to pages for the "easiest pair" and shifter-number points. Shimano's own dealer manual would not open (403), so shifter numbering rests on how-to pages only; see claim 6 and myths. Topic scope: a derailleur bike, a ring at the pedals, a stack of rings on the back wheel, a lever that moves the chain between back rings. Bikes with gears sealed inside the back hub (hub gears) work differently and are out of scope.

## 1. Is the title literally true?
- **"How do the gears on your bike work?"** (current) True, but it is the textbook framing the owner passes over, and the rules audit flags it. Don't use.
- **"Why does one gear click make the same hill feel easier?"** (11 words) Literally true with the obvious reading that the click goes toward the easy side (a click the other way makes the hill harder, as with "why does turning the tap make the water hot"). "Feel easier" is the honest word: the hill and your legs are unchanged, the push on each pedal turn gets lighter (claim 8), but the energy to lift you up it is the same (claim 7). "Easy" alone would over-claim, and "hill gets easier" would be false. It is personal (every rider has clicked it on a hill), it is a twist (nothing about you or the hill changed), it keeps slide 1 in spirit, and the answer is the whole topic. **Best.**
- **"Why does the easiest bike gear need the most pedal turns?"** (11 words) True for any given distance (claim 5 and the number in section 5). A real twist: "easy" is paid for in turns. Risk: "need" is only right per distance, not per minute (at a steady speed you also pedal faster in an easy gear), and a reader may hear "more turns" as "more work", which is false (claim 7). Fine as a second-topic title or the Go deeper thread; medium as the lead.
- **"Why does a smaller back ring make pedalling harder?"** True for the same pedal ring: a smaller back ring carries you farther per pedal turn and needs a harder push (claims 4, 5, 9). But it opens on a part the reader has never looked at, so it is a premise the reader doesn't hold (rule 9). It is also half-wrong if said alone, because at the pedals the opposite holds (a bigger pedal ring is harder; see myths). Don't use as the title; it is slide material inside the best title.
- **"Why does a bike have gears when your legs could just push?"** (12 words) True: single-speed bikes exist and people ride them. The honest answer is claim 10 (legs push best in a narrow range of pedalling speeds, and hills, flats and downhills each want a different one), which is a different spine from the hill slide 1. Plain and personal, a fair twist. Medium; use only if the owner drops the hill opening.
- **"Why do easy gears make you go slower?"** Not safe. At the same pedalling speed it is true; at the same road speed you simply pedal faster (claim 8); and on a steep hill the easy gear is what keeps you moving at all. The true form is "each pedal turn carries you a shorter way", which is a claim (5), not a title. Don't use "slower" as a flat statement.
- **"Why does your bike let you climb with a lighter push but no less work?"** True (claim 7) but abstract, and it opens on "work", a word that means something else to a newcomer. Don't use.
- **Best:** "Why does one gear click make the same hill feel easier?"

## 2. The answer in one sentence
The gear lever slides the chain onto a different-sized back ring, which changes how far each pedal turn carries you, so on a hill you trade a few hard pushes for many light ones while the lift to the top stays exactly the same.

## 3. Safe claims
1. A geared bike has one or more rings at the pedals, a stack of different-sized rings on the back wheel, and a chain joining the pedal ring to one back ring.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing (chainring to rear sprocket; "gear inches ... number of teeth in front chainring / number of teeth in rear sprocket"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derailleur_gears ("Most road bicycles have two chainrings"). Plain names: "pedal ring" and "back ring"; the engineering words are chainring and sprocket or cog.*
2. While you pedal, each turn of the back ring is one turn of the back wheel.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassette_(bicycle_part) (sprockets are connected to the hub the wheel turns); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing (distance per pedal turn follows from the tooth ratio and the wheel). Coasting is the exception, which is why the claim says "while you pedal".*
3. Each chain link fits one tooth, so a back ring with a third as many teeth as the pedal ring turns three times for each pedal turn: with 30 teeth at the pedals and 10 at the back, the wheel turns three times; with 30 and 30, once.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing (gear inches = wheel diameter x front teeth / rear teeth). Arithmetic: 30 / 10 = 3. The 30-and-10 pair is a made-up example, not a typical bike; label it "say" in the copy.*
4. A smaller back ring, with the same pedal ring, spins the back wheel more times for each pedal turn.
   *high — same formula; fewer rear teeth gives a bigger ratio. Wikipedia Bicycle gearing: "Metres of development corresponds to the distance traveled by the bicycle for one rotation of the pedals."*
5. More wheel turns for each pedal turn means each pedal turn carries you farther, so in an easy gear each turn takes you a shorter way.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing (metres of development). Real figures in section 5.*
6. The easiest pair is the biggest back ring with the smallest pedal ring, and the hardest pair is the smallest back ring with the biggest pedal ring.
   *high — https://bikexchange.com/how-to-shift-bike-gears-beginners-guide ("The large cog on the cassette is the lowest/easiest to pedal ... Lower or easier gear = the bigger cogs on the cassette, smaller chainring(s)"); https://www.sheldonbrown.com/gears.html (fetched as a gear-shifting guide: "The sprockets that are closer to the middle of the bike (small front, large rear) give the lower gears"); formula in claim 3. Say "pair" so the front and back are not mixed up.*
7. Lifting you and the bike to the top of a hill takes the same energy in any gear, and gears add no energy; they only change how that effort is split into pushes.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_performance (climbing power "= v_r m g s": speed, mass, gravity and slope, with no gear term in it); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_advantage ("An ideal mechanism transmits power without adding to and subtracting from it"). Real chains lose a little (Bicycle performance: derailleur gearing "reduces this by 1-7%"), so the sentence says "the energy to lift you", not "all the effort you spend".*
8. To go at the same speed, an easy gear means pedalling faster with a lighter push each time, and a hard gear means pedalling slower with a harder push.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing ("For a bicycle to travel at the same speed, using a lower gear ... requires the rider to pedal at a faster cadence, but with less force. Conversely, a higher gear ... provides a higher speed for a given cadence, but requires the rider to exert greater force").*
9. On the same hill, a gear that carries you three times as far with each pedal turn needs about three times the push on each pedal turn.
   *medium — arithmetic from claim 7: the lift to the top is fixed, so one turn that covers three times the hill has to do three times the lifting. "About" because of small losses and because the push varies round the turn. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_advantage (the device trades force against movement).*
10. Legs make power best in a narrow range of pedalling speeds, and gears let you stay in that range as the road changes.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing ("A cyclist's legs produce power optimally within a narrow pedalling speed range, or cadence. Gearing can be optimized to use this narrow range"); same page: "high gear when cycling downhill, a medium gear on a flat road, and a low gear when cycling uphill."*
11. Most riders pedal at about 60 to 90 turns a minute, which is about one to one and a half turns every second.
    *medium-high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadence_(cycling) ("60-80 r/min is a typical cadence for many recreational and utility cyclists"; "80-100 rpm is commonly recommended"; professionals about 90 on the flat and about 70 on steep climbs). The sources span 60 to 100, so 60 to 90 is the safe middle; never say "the best cadence is X".*
12. When you click the lever, a cable pulls a small arm at the back wheel, called the derailleur, and the arm slides the chain sideways onto another back ring.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derailleur_gears ("it moves the chain between rear sprockets while taking up chain slack"; "When a rider operates the lever while pedalling, the change in cable tension moves the chain-guide from side to side"). Plain: "derailleur" only if the topic needs it again; the arm is the spring arm that also keeps the loose chain tight.*

Spare claims, only if a slide needs them:
13. A derailleur only changes ring while the chain is moving, so you keep pedalling as you click.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derailleur_gears ("Derailleurs require the chain to be in movement in order to shift ... This usually requires the rider to be pedalling").*
14. Many bikes also have two or three rings at the pedals, so the gears multiply: three pedal rings and eight back rings give 24 gear pairs, but only about 16 to 18 of them are really different, because some pairs are almost the same.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing ("the number of possible gear ratios is 24 (=3x8, this is the number usually quoted in advertisements) ... usable ... 22 ... distinct gear ratios is typically 16 to 18"). Shifting the pedal ring changes the gear in bigger steps than a back-ring click (arithmetic from tooth counts; not a sourced sentence).*

### Numbered gears: what the copy may and may not say
No sourced universal rule. What I could read: one how-to page says that on its (Shimano-style) shifters "The number 1 corresponds to the biggest cog" at the back and "the little ring" at the front, so lower numbers are easier (https://coachlevi.com/cycling/complete-beginner-guide-to-bicycle-gears-shifting/); another page I found says the opposite for its bike, "the smaller the number, the harder"; cassettes are labelled by teeth, such as 11-34, not by position (Wikipedia, Cassette); and bikes with two or three pedal rings number those separately, so "gear 7" can mean a back-ring position, a pair, or a hub setting. So "gear 1 = biggest back ring" is a convention on some bikes, not a fact about bikes (I could not open Shimano's manual to settle which brands do what). **Safe plain sentence:** "Bikes number their gears in different ways, so use the size of the back ring, not the number: the biggest back ring is the easy one." If the copy wants numbers for a worked example, define them as a pretend bike: "Say we call the biggest back ring gear 1 and the smallest gear 7." That is a definition, not a claim, and the audit already shows the original copy does this. Avoid "low gear number = easy" as a bold line.

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"Gears make you stronger."** They don't add any energy or power (claim 7). They change the split between push and turns. A strong rider and a weak rider both climb the same hill by lifting the same weight to the same height; gears only let each of them choose a push they can manage. Say "the push gets lighter", never "you get more power".
- **"An easy gear means less work to climb."** The energy to lift you up is the same in any gear (claim 7). What changes is the push on each turn (lighter) and the number of turns (more). Easy gears can still tire you, because the heart and lungs take more of the load when the muscles push lightly and fast (Wikipedia Cadence: "a high cadence in low (easy) gear reduces the force to push down on the pedals and shifts more of the workload from the muscles to the cardiovascular system"). Never write "less effort" flatly; write "lighter push".
- **"A higher gear is always faster."** Speed is how many pedal turns you make a minute times how far each turn carries you. A hard gear carries you farther per turn, but if you can't turn the pedals in it (uphill, from a standstill) you go slower or stop, and on a steep downhill an easy gear can leave your legs spinning too fast to add anything. Say "a hard gear carries you farther with each turn", not "is faster". Same logic against "easy gears make you slower": it is only true at the same pedalling speed.
- **"Low gear number is always easy."** Numbering varies (see section above), and front and back rings are numbered or sized in opposite directions. Hub-gear bikes use their own numbers. Don't bold "low number = easy".
- **"A bigger ring is easier" (or "harder").** Both are false as a bare rule. At the back, a bigger ring is easier; at the pedals, a bigger ring is harder (claim 6). Always say "back ring" or "pedal ring" and name the pair.
- **"The lever changes the rings" or "makes your legs work differently".** Nothing about the rings or your legs changes; the lever only moves the chain onto a different ring.
- **"The chain gets longer or shorter."** It is the same length; the derailleur arm takes up the slack when the chain moves to a smaller back ring (Wikipedia, Derailleur gears).
- **"You can change gear while resting your feet."** You keep pedalling, or the chain won't move across (claim 13).
- **"A 21-speed bike has 21 different gears."** Some pairs overlap (spare claim 14).
- **Stairs: "leaping three steps costs extra effort in itself".** Don't push the stairs picture past "same height either way, harder for the legs" (see section 6). Don't claim total energy differences between steps and leaps.
- **Efficiency numbers** such as "derailleurs lose 1 to 7%" or "chains are 99% efficient". True on Wikipedia but they add a number with no payoff for a newcomer and invite "so gears do cost energy"; leave out.

## 5. One good number
In a common road-bike set-up (a small pedal ring of 34 teeth, a big one of 50, and back rings from 11 to 34 teeth), one pedal turn in the easiest pair (34 and 34) rolls the bike about 2 metres, and in the hardest pair (50 and 11) about 10 metres, four to five times as far. Everyday comparison: about the length of a bed in the easy gear, and about two cars nose to tail in the hard one. That is why a steep hill wants the bed: each turn lifts you only a little way up.
*Source: arithmetic. Distance per pedal turn = (pedal ring teeth / back ring teeth) x wheel rolling circumference (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing, "metres of development"). A standard 700x25c road tyre rolls 2,105 mm (https://cateye.com/intl/support/qa_search/001772.html, found by search; a bike-computer maker's chart, so medium on that link alone). 34/34 x 2.105 = 2.1 m; 50/11 x 2.105 = 9.6 m. 34/50 pedal rings: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crankset (compact road set-ups use 34 and 50); 11-34 back rings: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassette_(bicycle_part) ("10-speed bicycle cassette 11-34T", found in the article's example). Medium overall: it is one popular road set-up, not every bike, so say "on a road bike" and "about". A child's bike or a city bike gives smaller numbers.*
*Safer, simpler alternative, if the owner wants a number that needs no bike specs: Most riders pedal about one to one and a half turns every second (60 to 90 a minute, claim 11), the way a clock ticks about once a second. Or use the invented 30-teeth-to-10-teeth example (claim 3), labelled "say".*

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **Stairs: one step at a time or leaping three** (the current copy). Right picture for claims 7 and 9: same height gained either way; the big leap needs about three times the push, the small steps take three times the number. Misleads in four ways. (a) A stair has a fixed rise, so it suggests the gear changes how high you lift, when the hill's height is fixed and the gear changes only how many turns it is cut into. (b) It reads as "small steps are always better", because on a flat floor small steps are fine; the reader then asks "so why not always use the easy gear?", and the stairs have no answer (the answer is claim 10: you can't pedal fast enough, and each turn goes nowhere on the flat). (c) Leaping is a jump, a burst, while a hard gear is a steady heavy push. (d) A reader may think the pedals go up in steps. **Use only for "same top either way" and add the flat-road case on the next card, or replace it.**
- **Walking up a steep path in short quick steps or long strides.** Right for claims 5 and 9: same hill, same legs; long strides cover more ground per step but each one is a harder heave, short steps are lighter but you need more of them. It matches the bike one to one (a stride is a pedal turn; stride length is how far a turn carries you) and the reader has done it. Misleads only if the reader thinks a bike's gear changes the legs' reach: the legs go round the same circle and the chain does the changing. On the flat a long stride is also the faster one, which is the honest second half of the picture. **Best of the three: use, and say it is the same hill and the same legs.**
- **Carrying a pile of bricks up a ladder, one at a time or three at a time.** Right for "same total lifted, split into light loads or heavy loads". Misleads: carrying needs a trip back down, and the reader may think the easy way is slower in time only because of the walking back; it also suggests a rest between loads, which pedalling doesn't give. **Use only if the walking analogy is dropped; say the total bricks lifted is the same.**
- **A long gentle ramp versus a steep short one for lifting a heavy barrel.** Right physics, wrong picture: the hill itself already plays the ramp, so the reader maps the gear to the hill and loses the point. **Don't use.**
- **A seesaw or lever.** Misleads: "a longer lever lifts more" sounds like free strength, the "gears make you stronger" myth. **Don't use.**

## 7. Go deeper thread
Before chains, the pedals of the old penny-farthing were fixed straight to the hub of the huge front wheel, so there were no gears at all: one pedal turn was one wheel turn, and the only way to go farther per turn was a bigger wheel, up to about 60 inches across. The chain and a smaller back ring let a smaller wheel go as far as a big one, and that is the idea your gear lever still plays with. Even today the word "gear inches" means how wide a penny-farthing wheel would have to be to match your gear.
*Penny-farthing direct drive, bigger wheel instead of gears, 60-inch largest standard size, chain drive with a smaller sprocket letting a small wheel go as far as a big one: high, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny-farthing. "Gear inches corresponds to the diameter (in inches) of the main wheel of an old-fashioned penny-farthing bicycle with equivalent gearing": high, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_gearing. Derailleurs were introduced to the Tour de France in 1937 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derailleur_gears, high); do not say they were banned before that: not sourced here.*
