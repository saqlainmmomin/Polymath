## Slides

### Slide 1
**Headline:** Why does one gear click make the same hill feel easier?
**Setup:** Same hill, same legs.
**Text:** On a steep hill you push as hard as you can and barely move. Click the gear lever once and the same hill feels easier. Your legs aren't stronger, and the hill isn't flatter.
**Hook:** So what does the lever change at the back wheel?
**Picture:** A rider on a steep hill, pushing hard and barely moving. A finger clicks the gear lever, and the same rider on the same hill now rolls up it. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** GEAR LEVER · SAME HILL

### Slide 2
**Headline:** A smaller back ring spins the wheel more for each pedal turn
**Text:** The chain runs from the pedal ring to a stack of rings on the back wheel. The lever slides it onto another ring. Each link fits one tooth, so a ring with fewer teeth turns more times for the same length of chain.
**Hook:** So how far does one pedal turn carry you?
**Picture:** The pedal ring, the chain and the stack of back rings. One pedal turn pulls a stretch of chain past. On a bigger back ring it turns the wheel once, then the lever slides the chain to a smaller back ring and the same stretch of chain turns the wheel more times. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** PEDAL RING · CHAIN · BACK RINGS · GEAR LEVER

### Slide 3
**Headline:** A smaller back ring carries you farther with each pedal turn
**Text:** More wheel turns means each pedal turn carries you farther. With a bigger back ring, each turn takes you a shorter way.
**Hook:** So why does a shorter way per turn make the hill feel easier?
**Picture:** The bike seen from the side, along a road. One pedal turn with the smaller back ring: the bike rolls a long way. One pedal turn with the bigger back ring: it rolls a short way. The two distances are marked on the road. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** SMALLER BACK RING · BIGGER BACK RING · ONE PEDAL TURN · FARTHER · SHORTER WAY

### Slide 4
**Headline:** A shorter way per turn means a lighter push, and more turns to the top
**Text:** Think of walking up a steep path. Long strides are hard heaves, short quick steps are light, and you reach the same top either way. A pedal turn is like a stride, and the hill is the same height in every gear. So each turn lifts you only a little way up, and the push can be light.
**Hook:** But what about on flat road?
**Picture:** A steep path. A walker with long strides makes few, big heaves, and a second walker with short quick steps makes many light ones. Both reach the same top at the same height line. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** LONG STRIDES · SHORT QUICK STEPS · SAME TOP

### Slide 5
**Headline:** To go the same speed on flat road, a smaller back ring means slower pedalling
**Text:** To go the same speed, a smaller back ring means pedalling slower, with a harder push each time. On flat road that's fine, because legs push best in a narrow range of pedalling speeds.
**Bold:** A gear can't make the hill any lower. It only decides how many pushes it takes to climb it.
**Picture:** A rider on flat road. With the bigger back ring the legs spin fast and each push is light. After the lever clicks to the smaller back ring the legs slow down, each push gets harder, and the bike keeps the same speed. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** FLAT ROAD · SMALLER BACK RING · BIGGER BACK RING

### Go deeper
Before chains, the pedals of the old penny-farthing were fixed straight to the hub of the huge front wheel, so there were no gears at all: one pedal turn was one wheel turn, and the only way to go farther per turn was a bigger wheel, up to about 60 inches across. The chain and a smaller back ring let a smaller wheel go as far as a big one, and that is the idea your gear lever still plays with. Even today the word "gear inches" means how wide a penny-farthing wheel would have to be to match your gear.
