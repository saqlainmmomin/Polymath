# Fact sheet: Why does one Moon give most beaches two high tides a day?

Checked 2026-10-28. Sources fetched and quoted, not recalled: NOAA Ocean Service tide tutorial pages, Wikipedia (Tide, Tidal force, Tidal range, Bay of Fundy, Orbit of the Moon, Amphidromic point, Earth tide), National Geographic Education (Tide). Where a source's own wording is shaky it is flagged below.

## 1. Is the title literally true?
- **"How does the Moon move the oceans?"** True, but it is the textbook framing and it names the answer (the Moon). No twist. Don't use.
- **"Why does one Moon give most beaches two high tides a day?"** Literally true, with "most" doing honest work: NOAA says "most coastal areas, with some exceptions, experience two high tides and two low tides" and names the Gulf of Mexico as a one-high-tide place (https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides07_cycles.html). "A day" is the loose everyday wording: the true gap is 24 hours 50 minutes for two highs (https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/tidefrequency.html), so on about two calendar days a month a beach has only one high tide (arithmetic: highs about 12 h 25 min apart; see myths). Say "a day" in the title, never "every calendar day" in the copy. 12 words. **Best**: it is a twist (one Moon, two tides), it is personal (the beach), and the answer is the whole topic.
- **"Why are there two high tides a day with one Moon?"** Same truth, 11 words, drops "most", so it is a little over-claimed. Use only if the "most beaches" version is cut for length.
- **"Why does the sea rise twice a day, not once?"** True for most coasts, 10 words, does not spoil that the Moon is involved. Fallback if the owner wants the Moon kept out of the title.
- **"Why does high tide come about 50 minutes later each day?"** True as an average (NOAA: lunar day is 24 hours 50 minutes; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides05_lunarday.html), and it is the most daily-use title (anyone with a tide table). But the number is unfamiliar to a reader and the answer (the Moon is moving too) needs the bulge story first. Strong as the Go deeper thread or as a second topic, weaker as the lead. Medium.
- **"Why is the sea high on the side of Earth facing away from the Moon?"** Literally true only in the simple two-bulge picture (real tides are shaped by coasts and basins), and it opens on the single hardest, most myth-prone claim, with a premise the reader doesn't hold. Twisty but the riskiest. Don't use as the title; the idea is slide material inside the best title.
- **"Why does a beach flood and drain twice a day?"** True for most beaches. "Flood" suggests danger, and it is a description with no hidden reason beyond the best title's. Weaker.
- **"Why does the sea rise when the Moon is on the other side of the planet?"** Not safe. In real oceans high water arrives hours after the Moon is overhead or opposite (Wikipedia: observed tides are "strongly modified by ... the geometry of ocean basins, continental boundaries, bathymetry, the coriolis effect"). Don't use.
- **Best:** "Why does one Moon give most beaches two high tides a day?"

## 2. The answer in one sentence
The Moon pulls hardest on the sea nearest it, less on the ground, and least on the sea farthest away, which stretches the sea into two raised patches, one facing the Moon and one opposite, and as Earth turns once a day a beach is carried under each of them, giving two high tides.

## 3. Safe claims
1. On most coasts the sea rises and falls twice in a bit more than a day, giving two high tides and two low tides.
   *high — https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/tidefrequency.html ("two low tides and two high tides every lunar day, or 24 hours and 50 minutes"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/tide ("Most shorelines experience two high and two low tides within a twenty-four-hour period, though some areas have just one of each")*
2. A few places, such as the Gulf of Mexico, get only one high tide and one low tide a day.
   *high — https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides07_cycles.html (diurnal: "one high and one low tide every lunar day", found in "the Gulf of Mexico"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide*
3. The Moon pulls on everything on Earth, and it pulls harder on whatever is closer to it.
   *high — https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides03_gravity.html ("strongest on the side of the Earth that happens to be facing the moon, simply because it is closer")*
4. So the Moon pulls the sea on the near side harder than it pulls the ground, and pulls the sea on the far side less hard than it pulls the ground.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide ("a slightly stronger than average force on the side of the Earth facing the Moon, and a slightly weaker force on the opposite side"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force (tidal force is the difference in pull). "Average" there means the pull on Earth as a whole, so "the ground" is the plain word for it.*
5. That uneven pull stretches the sea into two raised patches, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side of Earth.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide ("a double tidal bulge along the Earth-Moon axis"); https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides03_gravity.html ("two bulges of water"). Do not say the two patches are the same size: Wikipedia says the near one is "somewhat larger".*
6. The two raised patches stay lined up with the Moon while Earth turns once a day beneath them.
   *high — https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides03_gravity.html ("the two bulges stay aligned with the moon as the Earth rotates"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide ("different points on Earth move through these bulges")*
7. Each time a beach is carried under a raised patch it has high tide, so it has a high tide about every 12 and a half hours.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide ("the time between semi-diurnal tides is not twelve but 12.4206 hours"); NOAA tidefrequency page above. Plain: "a bit over 12 hours".*
8. The Moon also travels round Earth in the same direction that Earth spins, so each day a beach needs about 50 extra minutes to catch up with it, and high tide comes about 50 minutes later each day.
   *high — https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides05_lunarday.html ("the moon revolves around the Earth in the same direction that the Earth rotates around its axis. So, it takes the Earth an extra 50 minutes to 'catch up' to the moon"). Say "about 50", never "exactly 50": it is an average and the local gap varies from day to day.*
9. The Sun pulls on the tide too, but only a bit less than half as hard as the Moon, because it is so much farther away.
   *high — https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html ("about half that of the moon"; the Sun is 390 times farther); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide (Sun's tidal pull about 46% of the Moon's)*
10. Out in the open ocean, the water rises and falls only about one metre between low tide and high tide.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_range ("The typical tidal range in the open ocean is about 1 metre"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide (theoretical Moon-only amplitude about 54 centimetres). It is the gap between low and high, not the height of the patch above flat sea.*
11. The shape of the sea floor and the coast changes the tide, which is why it is tiny in some seas, such as the Mediterranean, and huge in some bays.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide (observed tides "strongly modified by terrestrial factors, including the geometry of ocean basins, continental boundaries, bathymetry"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_range ("Some of the smallest tidal ranges occur in the Mediterranean, Baltic, and Caribbean Seas")*
12. In Canada's Bay of Fundy the sea has been measured rising and falling by more than 16 metres between low tide and high tide.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide ("a maximum range of 16.3 metres (53 ft)" at Burntcoat Head, November 1998). The Bay of Fundy page rounds the range to "about 16 metres", but the mean range there is 11.7 metres (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_range), so "the sea can rise and fall 16 metres" as an everyday figure overstates it. Use the measured-record wording.*

Spare claims, only if a slide needs them:
13. The solid ground rises and falls a little with the tide too, by up to about 30 to 40 centimetres, twice a day, and you cannot feel it.
    *medium — https://geodesy.science/?p=31457 ("up to about 30–40 centimeters twice daily"); https://jtbullitt.com/earthsound/solid_earth_tides/index.html ("about a foot high", "we don't ordinarily perceive these vibrations"). Wikipedia's Earth tide page gives "about 55 cm" amplitude at the equator, which disagrees; so do not give one exact figure. "About a ruler's length" is safe. The old copy's "up to about 30 centimetres" is the low end.*
14. The part of the Moon's pull that raises the sea is only about a ten-millionth of the pull that holds you to the ground.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide (lunar tidal acceleration about 1.1 x 10^-7 g)*
15. When the Sun, Moon and Earth line up, at new moon and full moon, their pulls add together and the tides are biggest, about twice a month.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide ("Approximately twice a month, around new moon and full moon")*
16. Earth and the Moon circle a shared point, and that point is inside Earth, about 4,670 km from Earth's centre.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon ("about 4,670 km ... from Earth's centre (about 73% of its radius)"). True but not needed to explain the tides; see myths.*

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"Left behind" as the reason for the far-side patch: risky as a bare phrase, safe only as a comparison.** Two ways it goes wrong. (a) The reader pictures the far water abandoned as the Moon pulls everything else away, or water "trying to keep going straight", which is the wording NOAA's own page uses ("inertia exceeds the gravitational force, and the water tries to keep going in a straight line", https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides03_gravity.html). (b) It slides into "Earth's spin flings the water out", which National Geographic states outright ("the rotational force of the Earth is stronger than the Moon's gravitational pull", https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/tide). That is the wrong cause: tidal forces "are not caused by the rotation" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force), and the far-side patch would exist without Earth's daily spin. **Safe version (claim 4):** the Moon pulls the ground harder than it pulls the sea on the far side, so the ground is pulled out from under that sea and the sea ends up raised there. One cause only: the pull is uneven.
- **The shared-orbit / centrifugal framing.** Wikipedia's Tide page explains the far-side patch as "a balance of the force of the Moon's gravity and centrifugal force", and it is not wrong, but "centrifugal" and "Earth circles a shared point" invite the spin myth above and need a rotating viewpoint a newcomer doesn't have. True and available (claim 16) but not the reason to give.
- **"The Moon pulls the water up, like a magnet."** The Moon's pull is not what lifts the sea against gravity; the sea piles up because the pull is uneven, and the uneven part is only about a ten-millionth of Earth's own pull (claim 14). Say "the sea slides toward", not "is lifted".
- **"Every coast has two high tides a day."** Some have one (Gulf of Mexico); some have two of different heights (the US west coast); some have almost none. NOAA tides07_cycles page.
- **"Two high tides every calendar day."** Not exactly. Highs are about 12 h 25 min apart, so about two days a month have only one high that day. Say "a bit over 12 hours apart" or "twice in a bit more than a day".
- **"High tide is when the Moon is overhead."** Real high water comes later than the Moon's passing, by an amount that depends on the place. The simple picture works for how many tides, not for exact times.
- **"The tide is a fixed hump that sits under the Moon and you can see it."** In open ocean it is only about a metre from low to high, spread over thousands of kilometres. Real tides are slow waves pushed round each ocean basin, circling points where the sea hardly rises at all (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphidromic_point).
- **"Rock is stiff, so it hardly moves."** Rock does move, up to 30 to 40 centimetres, a good share of the sea's own rise (claim 13). The old copy's line contradicts its own "more" note.
- **"The funnel shape squeezes the water up" as the whole reason for Bay of Fundy.** Wikipedia's reason is "tidal resonance in the funnel-shaped bay" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Fundy): the bay's own slosh timing matches the tide. A funnel is part of it, not all of it. Don't sell the funnel as the full cause.
- **"Spring tides are in spring."** They are not seasonal; the word means "spring up". Skip the term unless needed.
- **"The bulge on the Moon side is exactly as big as the far one."** The near one is somewhat larger (Wikipedia Tide).

## 5. One good number
The sea makes two high tides about 12 hours 25 minutes apart, which is why each day's high tide is about 50 minutes later than the day before: after about a week, a high tide that was at breakfast is at tea time. Everyday comparison: it is like a bus that runs every 24 hours 50 minutes while you try to catch it at the same time each day; you slip almost an hour later each time.
*Source: NOAA tidefrequency and lunar-day pages, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide (12.4206 hours). "About a week to go from breakfast to tea time" is arithmetic: 7 x 50 minutes is about 6 hours (medium; say "about"). Alternative, if the owner wants a number that explains why the tide is only a metre: the part of the Moon's pull that matters for the sea is about a ten-millionth of the pull that holds you down, so for a 70 kg person it changes their weight by less than half a grain of rice (7.7 mg; arithmetic from claim 14, with a rice grain of about 20 mg, so medium).*

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **Three runners in a line, all pulled forward by one strong magnet ahead, the front one hardest, the middle one less, the back one least.** Right picture for claims 4 and 5: the front runner pulls ahead of the middle runner, the back runner falls behind the middle runner, so the line stretches at both ends. Misleads if the reader thinks the back runner is not pulled at all, or if the next thought is "why doesn't Earth crash into the Moon?" (Earth and the Moon circle each other, and that is a separate topic). **Use, and say all three are pulled, just by different amounts; the middle runner is the ground.**
- **Clock hands.** The minute hand returns to the top every hour but needs a few extra minutes to catch the slowly moving hour hand. Right picture for the 50 extra minutes: Earth is the faster turner catching a slower-moving Moon. Misleads only on the size (a clock needs about 5 extra minutes, Earth 50) and if the reader pictures the Moon as fixed. **Use only if the 50 minutes is in the paragraph, and say "catching up", not "the same".**
- **Pushing a swing at the right beat.** Right for Bay of Fundy resonance: small pushes at the right rhythm build a big swing. Misleads if the reader thinks the Moon pushes the bay directly like a hand on a swing; it is the sea's slosh that builds up. **Use only for the Bay of Fundy slide, one line.**
- **Squeezing a water balloon from two sides.** Misleads: it suggests two pushes, one from each side, when there is one Moon and an uneven pull. **Don't use.**

## 7. Go deeper thread
A tide is really a very slow wave that moves through the sea, and in each ocean it circles around a point where the sea hardly rises or falls at all, which is called an amphidromic point. That is why high tide never lines up neatly with the Moon overhead, why some coasts get one tide a day, and why the sea in the Mediterranean hardly moves while the Bay of Fundy swings 16 metres. In 1687 Isaac Newton explained tides as the pull of the Moon and Sun on the sea.
*Slow wave and crest/trough: high, https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/tides.html ("very long-period waves that move through the ocean"); amphidromic points: high, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphidromic_point ("little or no difference in sea height between high tide and low tide", "rotates around the amphidromic point"); Newton 1687: high, https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html. Do not call Newton "the first": not sourced here.*
