# Moon tides, stage 2 (paragraph for owner approval)

**Title:** Why does one Moon give most beaches two high tides a day?
**Setup:** There's only one Moon, and it's on one side of the sky at a time.

**Paragraph:**
[1] The Moon pulls the sea toward it, so you'd expect one high tide a day. But most beaches get two. Where does the second one come from? [2] The Moon pulls harder on whatever is closer. So it pulls the sea facing it harder than it pulls the ground, and the sea on the far side less hard than the ground. [3] Picture three runners in a line, all pulled by one magnet ahead, the front one hardest and the back one least. The front runner is the near sea, the middle runner is the ground, and the back runner is the far sea. The near sea is pulled ahead of the ground, and the ground is pulled ahead of the far sea. So the sea piles up at both ends, in two bulges. [4] The bulges stay lined up with the Moon while Earth turns once a day beneath them, so your beach passes under one, then the other. That's two high tides, a bit over twelve hours apart. [5] The Moon also moves round Earth the way Earth spins. So each day your beach needs about 50 extra minutes to catch up with it, and high tide comes about 50 minutes later.

**Bold line:** One Moon stretches the sea at both ends, and Earth turns your beach through each end.

**Go deeper:** A tide is really a very slow wave moving through the sea, and in each ocean it circles a point where the sea hardly rises or falls at all, called an amphidromic point. That is why high tide never lines up neatly with the Moon overhead, and why some coasts get one tide a day. In 1687 Isaac Newton explained tides as the pull of the Moon and Sun on the sea.

**Most likely to lose a reader:** slide 3, the runners picture has to turn "pulled least" into a far-side bulge.
