### Slide 2
**Headline:** The Moon pulls the near sea harder than the ground, and the far sea less
**Text:** The Moon pulls harder on whatever is closer. So the sea facing it, the near sea, is pulled harder than the ground. The sea on the far side, the far sea, is pulled less hard than the ground.
**Hook:** So where does all that pulling make the sea pile up?
**Picture:** Earth seen from above with the Moon to one side. Three arrows from the Moon end on the near sea, the ground and the far sea, the longest on the near sea, the shortest on the far sea. Loops by itself.

### Slide 3
**Headline:** The sea piles up on both sides of Earth, in two bulges
**Text:** Picture three runners in a line. One magnet ahead pulls them all, the front one hardest and the back one least. The front runner is the near sea, the middle one the ground, the back one the far sea. The near sea is pulled ahead of the ground, and the ground is pulled ahead of the far sea. So the sea piles up on both sides of Earth, in two bulges.
**Hook:** So what happens to your beach as Earth turns?
**Picture:** Three runners in a line pulled toward a magnet: the gaps open up. Then the runners change into the near sea, the ground and the far sea around Earth, and the sea rises into a bulge at both ends. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** NEAR SEA · GROUND · FAR SEA · TWO BULGES · MOON

### Slide 4
**Headline:** As Earth turns under the bulges, your beach gets two high tides
**Text:** The bulges stay lined up with the Moon while Earth turns once a day beneath them. Your beach passes under one, then the other. That's two high tides, a bit over twelve hours apart.
**Hook:** But why does each day's high tide come a little later?
**Picture:** Earth from above, turning, with the two bulges fixed toward the Moon. A dot for your beach rides round: high tide in one bulge, low tide between, high tide in the other. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** BULGE · BULGE · YOUR BEACH · HIGH TIDE · LOW TIDE

### Go deeper
A tide is really a very slow wave moving through the sea, and in each ocean it circles a point where the sea hardly rises or falls at all, called an amphidromic point. That is why high tide never lines up neatly with the Moon overhead. In 1687 Isaac Newton explained tides as the pull of the Moon and Sun on the sea.
