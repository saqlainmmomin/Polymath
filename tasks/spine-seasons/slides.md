## Slides

### Slide 1
**Headline:** Why is it summer when Earth is farthest from the Sun?
**Setup:** Summer feels like the Sun has come closer.
**Text:** But in early July, while Europe and the USA have summer, Earth is at its farthest from the Sun. In early January, in their winter, it's at its closest.
**Hook:** So is it much farther away in July than in January?
**Picture:** Earth's path round the Sun, drawn as the near-circle it is. Earth sits at the far side in July, and its north half wears a little sun hat labelled SUMMER. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** JULY: FARTHEST · JANUARY: CLOSEST · SUMMER UP NORTH IN JULY

### Slide 2
**Headline:** Earth is only about 3 percent farther in July than in January
**Text:** And when Australia has summer, Canada has winter, at the same distance from the Sun. So distance can't be it.
**Hook:** So if it isn't distance, what is it?
**Picture:** Two paths of footsteps to the Sun: 100 steps in January, 103 in July. Then one Earth with Australia in summer and Canada in winter, at the same moment. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** JANUARY: 100 STEPS · JULY: 103 STEPS · AUSTRALIA: SUMMER · CANADA: WINTER

### Slide 3
**Headline:** Each half of Earth takes a turn leaning toward the Sun
**Text:** Earth leans about 23 degrees from upright, like a classroom globe, and the lean always points toward the same faraway spot. So in June the north half leans toward the Sun, and in December, on the other side of its trip, it leans away. The south half does the opposite.
**Hook:** But why would leaning toward the Sun make it warmer?
**Picture:** Earth travels round the Sun with its lean drawn as an arrow that never turns. In June the north half faces the Sun and is lit; in December, across the path, it faces away. This is the weak step: the picture must make "the lean never turns" obvious. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** THE LEAN NEVER TURNS · JUNE: NORTH LEANS IN · DECEMBER: NORTH LEANS AWAY

### Slide 4
**Headline:** Leaning toward the Sun packs its light into a smaller patch
**Text:** Shine a torch straight down at a table and you get a small, bright spot. Tilt it, and the same light spreads into a long, dim patch. The half leaning toward the Sun gets light more like the straight beam, so each bit of ground warms more.
**Hook:** Does the lean change anything else?
**Picture:** A torch over a table. Straight down: a small, bright spot. Tilted: the same light spread into a long, dim patch. The topic's one hands-on control: drag to tilt the torch.
**Picture labels:** STRAIGHT: SMALL, BRIGHT SPOT · TILTED: LONG, DIM PATCH

### Slide 5
**Headline:** The lean also makes summer days longer
**Text:** It puts more of that half on the sunny side as Earth spins. In London, a June day is about twice as long as a December one.
**Bold:** Summer doesn't come when Earth is closer to the Sun. It comes when your half leans toward it.
**Picture:** Earth spinning with a day side and a night side. A dot for London spends most of its spin in daylight in June and most of it in the dark in December. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** DAY · NIGHT · LONDON IN JUNE · LONDON IN DECEMBER

### Go deeper
At the North Pole, the Sun stays up for about six months, then come months of twilight and dark. The same lean does it.
