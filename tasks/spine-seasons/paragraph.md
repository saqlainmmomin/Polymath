# Seasons, stage 2 (paragraph for owner approval)

**Title:** Why is it summer when Earth is farthest from the Sun?
**Setup:** Summer feels like the Sun has come closer.

**Paragraph:**
[1] But in early July, while Europe and the USA have summer, Earth is at its farthest from the Sun. In early January, in their winter, it's at its closest. So is it much farther away in July than in January? [2] No, only about 3 percent farther. And when Australia has summer, Canada has winter, at the same distance from the Sun. So distance can't be it. What is? [3] Earth leans about 23 degrees from upright, like a classroom globe, and the lean always points toward the same faraway spot. So in June the north half leans toward the Sun, and in December, on the other side of its trip, it leans away. The south half does the opposite. But why would leaning toward the Sun make it warmer? [4] Shine a torch straight down at a table and you get a small, bright spot. Tilt it, and the same light spreads into a long, dim patch. Sunlight works the same way: the half leaning toward the Sun gets light more like the straight beam, packed onto a smaller patch, so each bit of ground warms more. [5] The lean also puts more of that half on the sunny side as Earth spins, so its days are longer. In London, a June day is about twice as long as a December one.

**Bold line:** Summer doesn't come when Earth is closer to the Sun. It comes when your half leans toward it.

**Go deeper:** At the North Pole, the Sun stays up for about six months, then come months of twilight and dark. The same lean does it.

**Slide most likely to lose a reader:** [3]. "The lean always points the same way" and "the north half leans toward the Sun in June, away in December" sound like a contradiction until you see Earth travel round the Sun with its lean fixed. Both cold reads stalled here. The words alone can't carry it; the picture must show Earth moving round its path with the lean arrow never turning. Runner-up: [5], where "more of that half on the sunny side" needs the day/night picture.

**Both causes?** The torch carries the answer by itself, and the bold line works without day length. I kept day length because the owner settled on two causes and because long summer evenings are something every reader has felt. It costs a slide. If you want it shorter, fold [5] into one closing sentence of [4] ("The lean also gives that half longer days, so the Sun has more hours to warm it") and drop London: 4 slides, about 160 words.
