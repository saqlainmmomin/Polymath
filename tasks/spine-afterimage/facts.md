# Fact sheet: Why do you see spots after looking at a bright light?

Checker note: every URL below was opened this session. Where a source only gave a short or summarised line, confidence is marked medium.

## 1. Is the title literally true?
Yes, with one fix. After a bright flash or bulb people do see a spot, but it is not one thing. It goes in two steps: first a short bright ghost of the light (the "positive" phase, under half a second), then a longer dim or dark patch, often with fleeting colours (the "negative" phase). The dark patch is the part that "follows your eyes" for seconds, so "spot" is right for what readers remember. Colour: after a bright WHITE flash the long phase is dim/dark with passing colours; the "opposite colour" rule is documented for COLOURED light (red gives green, blue gives yellow), not for white light, so the copy must not say a white flash leaves a "complementary colour". "Spots" (plural) is loose: it is one patch shaped like the light. Title "spots" is fine for a feed; the copy should say one dark patch.
*Sources: https://annex.exploratorium.edu/xref/phenomena/afterimage.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterimage ; https://www.illusionsindex.org/i/negative-afterimages*

## 2. The answer in one sentence
A very bright light makes the light-sensing cells it hit in your eye less sensitive for a little while, so when you look at something normal that patch of your eye reports less than its neighbours and you see a dim spot that moves wherever your eyes go.

## 3. Safe claims
1. The retina is a light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye; its cells turn light into electrical signals that travel along the optic nerve to the brain, which turns them into the pictures you see.
   *high — https://www.nei.nih.gov/learn-about-eye-health/healthy-vision/how-eyes-work*
2. The retina has two kinds of light-sensing cell: rods, which work in dim light and see no colour, and cones, which handle colour, bright light and fine detail.
   *high — https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fas/psych/glossary/cones_and_rods ; https://www.allaboutvision.com/resources/human-interest/afterimage/*
3. Each eye has about 120 million rods and 6 to 7 million cones.
   *high — https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fas/psych/glossary/cones_and_rods*
4. An intense light makes the light-sensing cells it lands on less sensitive for a while.
   *high — https://annex.exploratorium.edu/xref/phenomena/afterimage.html ("loss of sensitivity of the receptors" from intense light) ; https://www.illusionsindex.org/i/negative-afterimages ("the sensitivity of the activated photoreceptors in that particular region will decrease")*
5. When you then look at a plain surface, the less sensitive patch answers less than the cells around it, so you see a dim area.
   *high — https://explorer.technorama.ch/en/experiment/afterimage ("the desensitized area does not respond as well to new light input") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterimage ("the dim area that seems to float before one's eyes after briefly looking into a light source, such as a camera flash")*
6. After a flash you first see a bright ghost of it for about a second or less, and then it turns dark, often with fleeting colours.
   *high — https://annex.exploratorium.edu/xref/phenomena/afterimage.html ; positive phase "less than half a second": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterimage*
7. After staring at a coloured light, the dark patch tends to look like the opposite colour: red gives green, blue gives yellow.
   *high — https://www.illusionsindex.org/i/negative-afterimages ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterimage (green stimulus gives magenta)*
8. The dark patch starts in the retina: it does not carry over from one eye to the other, so it is made in the eye, not in the brain.
   *high — https://explorer.technorama.ch/en/experiment/afterimage ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3381099/ ("afterimage signals are generated in the retina")*
9. The patch stays fixed on the same bit of your retina, so when your eyes move it seems to move with them.
   *high — https://www.scienceofintelligence.de/the-ghosts-we-see-how-afterimages-reveal-why-the-world-appears-stable/*
10. The dark patch usually lasts several seconds and sometimes 30 seconds or more, then fades as the cells recover.
    *high — https://www.allaboutvision.com/resources/human-interest/afterimage/ ("several seconds") ; https://explorer.technorama.ch/en/experiment/afterimage ("30 seconds or longer") ; recovery: https://optimax.co.uk/blog/photo-flash-damage-eyes ("returns to normal as the pigment regenerates")*
11. A normal afterimage is a natural part of vision, and a camera flash is very safe for eyes because it lasts only about 1/400 of a second.
    *high — https://www.allaboutvision.com/resources/human-interest/afterimage/ ; https://optimax.co.uk/blog/photo-flash-damage-eyes*
12. Looking straight at the Sun is different: it can permanently damage the retina, it does not hurt while it happens because the retina has no pain nerves, and the vision problems show up hours later.
    *high — https://AAO.ORG/eye-health/tips-prevention/think-you-hurt-your-eyes-watching-eclipse (symptoms within 4 to 6 hours, some after 12; "no treatment"; many recover in 3 to 6 months, some keep a small blind spot)*

Held back (not safely true or not fully verified, do not use):
- "You still see it with eyes closed": Wikipedia says a negative afterimage "can also be achieved by the viewer closing their eyes", but that is a one-line statement about how to view it, and the page-fetch gave no detail. Medium confidence, not in the 12. If the owner wants a closed-eyes beat, have a human test it and say "many people see it" only after that.
- "Blinking does not remove it": no opened source states it directly. Dropped.

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"The flash burns a hole in your eye."** A normal afterimage is temporary and a camera flash (about 1/400 s) is very safe. Only looking at the Sun (or similar) causes real retinal damage.
- **"The light is stuck in your eye."** Nothing is stored. The cells answer less for a while; the dim patch is a weaker signal, not leftover light. (The short bright phase is the cells still firing after the light is gone, under half a second.)
- **"The cells run out of chemicals" / "the cells get tired".** Sources use "tired/fatigued" and "bleached pigment", but the mechanism is contested (see below). Safe wording: "the cells it hit answer less for a while". Do not say they "ran out".
- **"Other cones pick up the slack."** One source says this; not established. Do not use.
- **"The afterimage is in the brain."** The dark patch starts in the retina. Do not say "it's all in your head" either; the brain only reads what the eye sends (and may adjust it).
- **"A white flash leaves a coloured spot of the opposite colour."** The opposite-colour rule is for coloured light. After white, the colours are fleeting and varied.
- **"The spot is a real object out there / it is stuck on the wall."** It is on the retina, so it moves when you look elsewhere.

CONTESTED (writer must stay on the safe side of both):
- **Bleaching versus other retinal changes.** Older sources (Exploratorium, Optimax) say the dark phase comes from bleaching of the light-sensitive pigment. A 2012 study in primate retina (Current Biology, via PMC3381099) found slower adaptation in retinal ganglion cells (cells after the light-sensing cells) can account for colour afterimages, so "pigment bleaching is the cause" is not settled for all afterimages. Illusions Index also says the cortex plays a role in negative afterimages. Hence claims 4, 5 and 8 are worded as "cells in the retina answer less" and "starts in the retina", not "the pigment is bleached".

## 5. One good number
A camera flash lasts about 1/400 of a second, but the dark patch it leaves lasts several seconds, at least a thousand times longer than the flash itself.
*Sources: https://optimax.co.uk/blog/photo-flash-damage-eyes (1/400 s) ; https://www.allaboutvision.com/resources/human-interest/afterimage/ (several seconds). The "at least a thousand times" is arithmetic: 3 s divided by 1/400 s is 1,200. Writer may say "a blink-fast flash, a spot that lasts seconds" without the ratio.*
Alternative number if preferred: about 120 million rods and 6 to 7 million cones per eye (https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fas/psych/glossary/cones_and_rods), but there is no verified everyday comparison for it.

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **A tired hand after squeezing hard.** Squeeze, let go, and for a moment your grip is weaker, then it recovers. Right intuition: used a lot, then answers less for a while, then back to normal. Safe, as long as the copy does not say the cells "ran out of energy" (see myths). Not perfect: the cells are not working hard in the dark, they are responding less.
- **Walking from a bright street into a dim room.** You can barely see for a moment, then it clears. Right intuition: the same eye cells being less sensitive after bright light, then recovering (rods need 30 minutes or longer to fully adapt to darkness: https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fas/psych/glossary/cones_and_rods). Safe but it is a second effect, so a reader may conflate it with the spot; use only if the copy says "the same kind of recovery". Medium.
- **An overexposed phone-camera photo / sensor with a ghost.** Misleads: a sensor does not keep a ghost and does not "recover" in seconds; readers may think the light is stored in the eye. Not safe.
- **A camera flash "stamping" a picture.** Rejected: it is the "light is stuck" myth.

## 7. Go deeper thread
Look at the spot against a far wall and it seems big; against your hand it seems small. The patch on your retina does not change size; only your brain's guess at its size changes.
*high — https://explorer.technorama.ch/en/experiment/afterimage ("The actual afterimage does not change size. The only thing changing is your interpretation of its size.")*
