# Afterimage, stage 2 (paragraph for owner approval)

**Title:** Why do you see spots after looking at a bright light?
**Setup:** The flash is over in an instant. The spot it leaves is not.

**Paragraph:**
[1] Someone takes your photo with a flash, and for the next few seconds a dark spot floats in the middle of everything you look at. Look at the wall: it's there. Look at the floor: it's there too. But there's nothing on the wall or the floor. So where is the spot? [2] It's in your eye. At the back of each eye is a layer of cells that turn light into signals for your brain. The flash hits a patch of those cells so hard that they get tired out. For a little while, they respond less to light than the cells around them. [3] Then you look at a plain wall. The cells around the patch send signals for the wall as usual, but the tired cells send weaker ones. Your brain gets a normal picture with a dark spot in it. [4] The spot follows your eyes because it's tied to that part of your eye, not to anything out there. A few seconds later, when the cells recover, it fades.

**Bold line:** The spot isn't in the room. It's a patch of your eye that got tired out by the flash.

**Go deeper:** Look at the spot against a far wall and it seems big; against your hand it seems small. The patch on your eye never changes size. Only your brain's guess at its size does.

**Slide most likely to lose a reader:** [3]. "Send weaker signals" is abstract; "tired" helps. The picture has to show a grid of eye cells, a few dimmed, and the wall picture with a dim patch built from exactly those cells. Runner-up: [2], which gives the reader the retina in one sentence; the picture must show it as a layer at the back of the eye.
