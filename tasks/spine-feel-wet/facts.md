# Fact sheet: Can your skin feel wet when it is dry?

Checked 2026-10-28. Sources fetched and quoted, not recalled: Bentley 1900, "The Synthetic Experiment", American Journal of Psychology 11(3), 405-425 (read in the original scan, archive.org item sim_american-journal-of-psychology_1900-04_11_3, pp. 419-425); Filingeri, Fournet, Hodder and Havenith 2014, "Why wet feels wet?", J Neurophysiol 112(6), 1457-1469, PMID 24944222 (abstract read via Europe PMC); Filingeri and Ackerley 2017, J Neurophysiol review "The biology of skin wetness perception..." (PMC5384974); Filingeri et al 2015 tight-fit clothing study (PMC4580857); a 2021 fingerpad wetness study (PMC8356767); ScienceDaily and Neuroscience News write-ups of the 2014 study; Wikipedia (Skin, Thermoreceptor, Hygroreception, Hypothermia, List of thermal conductivities, TRPM8); Europe PMC abstracts for fruit-fly and mosquito humidity sensing (Curr Biol 2016; Neuron 2023; PNAS 2024; Curr Opin Neurobiol 2026). Not found or blocked: the full text of the 2014 paper (journals.physiology.org returned 403; PubMed showed a captcha), so its details come from the abstract and the 2017 review; the National Weather Service "25 times" line came from a search excerpt, not an opened page; the "5 of 9 people felt wet from cold dry stimuli" figure is from a search summary of a Filingeri and Havenith paper I could not open (medium). No source found for the washing-line example in the current copy.

Scope: how human skin gives the feeling of wet, in everyday settings (cold water, damp cloth, sweat). Not in scope: wet-air humidity (muggy weather), the full nerve wiring, sight and sound cues, babies learning wet.

## 1. Is the title literally true?
- **"How do you know something is wet?"** (current) True but not safe: "know" invites the answer "I see it". Sources say vision helps confirm wet (2017 review: "Input from the other senses, especially vision, plays a role in confirming cutaneous wetness processing"). It is the textbook "how X works" framing with no twist. Don't use.
- **"Can your skin feel wet when it is dry?"** (9 words) Literally true. Bentley 1900: a dry finger inside a thin rubber sheath, lowered into water, felt wet ("the subjects at first refusing to believe that the finger was not actually wetted"). The 2017 review adds that "illusion of wetness could be induced in blindfolded naive individuals exposed to nonnoxious cold-dry stimuli", with no moisture on the skin. A plain yes/no question with no premise the reader must hold. Needs cold and touch, so the slides must say what makes it work, not "always". **Best.**
- **"Why does cold water feel wetter than warm water?"** True: in the 2014 lab test "individuals perceived warm-wet and neutral-wet stimuli as significantly less wet than cold-wet stimuli, although these were characterized by the same moisture content." Personal and daily-use, but only a mild twist, and it carries a premise ("wetter") some readers will not hold; warm water that moves over skin still feels wet (2017 review). Fallback.
- **"Does your skin have a sense of wet?"** The honest answer is no, and the title gives that away. "Sense of wet" is also loose: people do sense wet, they just have no sensor made for it. Weaker.
- **"Why does a dry finger feel wet in cold water?"** Not safe: it states a result as an everyday fact, but it only happens with a thin sheath between finger and water, which nobody does daily; and it assumes the reader already knows it. Don't use.
- **"Why do you need cold to feel wet?"** Not safe. Cold is not strictly needed: sweat-soaked clothing feels wet through touch alone (claim 11), and warm water still feels wet, just less. Don't use.
- **Best:** "Can your skin feel wet when it is dry?" Literal truth rests on claims 9 and 10.

## 2. The answer in one sentence
Your skin has no sensor made just for wet; it has cold sensors and touch sensors, and your brain builds the feeling of wet when it gets a cold signal and a close, even touch together, so you can feel wet even when no water is touching your skin.

## 3. Safe claims
1. Your skin has tiny nerve endings (sensors) that react to cold, to warmth, to touch and pressure, and to pain, among other things.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin ("contains a variety of nerve endings that jump to heat and cold, touch, pressure, vibration, and tissue injury"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoreceptor ("a non-specialised sense receptor, or more accurately the receptive portion of a sensory neuron, that codes absolute and relative changes in temperature"; cold and warm are separate receptors in mammals). Say "such as" or "among others", never "four sensors": skin has more kinds (vibration, itch) and the current picture's list of four invites a quibble. Pressure and touch are one word for this topic.*
2. Cold sensors in your skin react when your skin gets colder.
   *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoreceptor (cold receptors fire more during cooling, fire less during warming; page text paraphrased in my fetch, not a clean quote); https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5384974/ ("evaporation-induced skin cooling and nonnoxious cold sensations underpin our skin wetness sensitivity").*
3. Scientists have found no sensor in human skin that is made just for wetness.
   *high — Filingeri et al 2014 abstract (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24944222/, read via Europe PMC): "humans are not provided with specific skin receptors for sensing wetness"; PMC8356767: "Human skin has not yet been found to contain hygroreceptors, a specific receptor for wetness in the skin"; PMC5384974: "there is no evidence that humans possess a specific sensory receptor for the transduction of skin wetness into neural signals". Wording is "has found none", which is true however the research goes; "your skin has no wet sensor" is also what the abstract itself says flat out, so either is fine. Do not use "cannot" or "never".*
4. Water takes heat out of your skin about 25 times faster than air does.
   *medium-high — the ratio of thermal conductivity: water 0.5918, air 0.024 to 0.025 W per metre per kelvin (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_thermal_conductivities), which is about 24 times; the National Weather Service line, found by search: "water conducts body heat away up to 25 times faster than air of the same temperature"; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothermia ("Heat is lost much more quickly to water than to air"). It is the ratio for the material, not a measured rate for a wet finger in a breeze, so say "about 25 times" and only for water against air at the same temperature.*
5. A wet thing on your skin makes your skin colder, because water carries heat away and because it dries off and cools you as it does.
   *high — PMC5384974: "conductive and evaporative heat transfer occurring when moisture contacts the skin determines the thermal component". In copy say "water takes heat out of your skin", and leave the word "evaporates" out unless the topic needs it twice.*
6. A wet thing also gives your skin a close, even touch.
   *medium-high — Bentley 1900, p. 419-420: "a close-fitting pressure, strongest at the tip and at the ring ... it creeps up and down"; p. 424: "the chief difficulty in synthetizing liquidity has been the production of an even, close-fitting pressure". A 1900 study with a few observers, and the 2017 review says "it is not known which mechanoreceptive afferents encode the exact properties of wetness". So claim only "a close, even touch", never which nerve carries it, and never "all over at once".*
7. When your skin gets both a cold signal and that touch, your brain puts them together into the feeling of wet.
   *medium-high — Filingeri 2014 abstract: "we developed the first neurophysiological model of cutaneous wetness sensitivity centered on the multisensory integration of cold-sensitive and mechanosensitive skin afferents"; when cold and touch sensitivity was reduced, "wetness perception was significantly reduced". Bentley 1900 p. 420, independently: "where either temperature or pressure was lacking 'wetness' was also lacking". "Medium-high" because the 2014 paper itself calls the integration its tested hypothesis and a model, not a settled fact. "Puts together" is safer than "adds".*
8. In a lab test, the same amount of water felt wetter when it was cold than when it was warm.
   *high — Filingeri 2014 abstract (quoted in section 1); https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/10/141001133416.htm ("Wet perception increased as temperature decreased"; 13 male college students tested). Small test group, so say "in a lab test", not "always".*
9. In 1900, a psychologist put a dry finger inside a thin rubber sheath and lowered it into water; it felt wet, and people at first refused to believe the finger was dry.
   *high for the report — Bentley 1900 p. 424: "A thin rubber sheath was drawn over the finger, and the finger lowered into the liquid. The perception of liquidity here was complete, the subjects at first refusing to believe that the finger was not actually wetted. The perception lasted (with cold water) for some time after withdrawal." Bentley's other set-up (a dry rubber-tissue pocket in water) gave "a decided perception of wetness" with ice-cold water (zero centigrade). He worked with "L, S, B, and several others": a handful of people, so say "in 1900, people", not "everyone". Secondary: https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/node/11575 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5384974/ both repeat it.*
10. With warmer water in the same set-up, it felt less wet, and lukewarm water felt barely wet or not wet at all.
    *high — Bentley 1900 p. 424: "Warm water gave a less decided wetness, and lukewarm a very faint liquidity or none at all." Same direction as claim 8 in the 2014 test. Do not turn this into "warm water doesn't feel wet" (see myths).*
11. Touch alone can change how wet something feels, with the same real wetness.
    *high — Filingeri et al 2015 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4580857/): "the reduced skin friction generated by the TIGHT-FIT ensemble lowered significantly the level of perceived skin wetness", while there were "no differences" in sweat made; "perception of skin wetness is primarily driven by the degree of tactile interaction between skin, sweat and clothing". Spare: use only if a slide needs the touch half proved on its own. Note it is from sweat and exercise, not cold water.*

Spare claims, only if a slide needs them:
12. Some people felt wet from a cold, dry object on bare skin.
    *medium — PMC5384974: "illusion of wetness could be induced in blindfolded naive individuals exposed to nonnoxious cold-dry stimuli"; a search summary of Filingeri and Havenith gives 5 of 9 people felt wet, 4 did not (not opened). Say "some people". Bentley found cold alone gave no wetness in his liquid tests (p. 420), so the two disagree on cold alone: do not write "cold alone is enough" or "touch alone is enough" for cold.*
13. Fruit flies and mosquitoes have sensors for how damp the air is, on their antennae.
    *high for fruit flies: Curr Biol 2016 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27161501/, abstract via Europe PMC): "specialized neurons innervating pore-less sensilla of the sacculus, a unique invagination of the third antennal segment"; the 2017 review: "Humidity-sensitive sensory organs are present in numerous insects, including fruit flies and cockroaches". Mosquitoes: PNAS 2024 "Both classes of hygrosensors innervate multiple antennal sensilla"; Neuron 2023 "Humidity sensors that alert mosquitoes to nearby hosts and egg-laying sites" ("Ir93a-dependent sensors drive human host proximity detection and blood-feeding behavior"); Curr Opin Neurobiol 2026: "Both Ir68a and Ir93a mutants are unable to find water to lay eggs". These sense water vapour in the air, not the feeling of a wet surface. Fine for Go deeper.*
14. Your skin's cold sensors can be fooled with no cold present: menthol switches on the same sensor that cold does.
    *high — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRPM8 ("activated by cold temperatures and cooling agents, such as menthol and icilin"). Spare; a different mechanism (a chemical triggers a real sensor) from wet (the brain combines two), so do not use it as the explanation, only as a Go-deeper if the insect thread is dropped.*

## 4. Myths to avoid
- **"Your skin has a sensor for wet."** The sources say it does not (claim 3). The myth is the starting belief; slide 1 should break it, not assume it.
- **"Wet is just cold."** Not what the sources say: Bentley found cold alone or touch alone did not give wet in his liquid tests (p. 420), and the 2014 paper models cold and touch together. Say "cold and touch together", not "cold".
- **"Warm water doesn't feel wet."** Not true: warm water feels less wet (claims 8 and 10), and the 2017 review says warm moisture can still be sensed "unless movement" is absent; a warm bath feels wet. Say "less wet", never "not wet".
- **"Water is always colder than your skin."** Not true (a hot bath is not). This is why the current copy said "usually". Write it as a condition that is simply true: "water that is cooler than your skin takes heat out of it fast".
- **"The brain guesses wet from cold and touch," as if it were a coin toss.** The sources describe "perceptual inference" and a "model", not a random guess; and "it has been proposed that we 'learn' to perceive" wet is a proposal in the abstract, so do not write "your brain has learned". "Builds", "puts together" and "combines" fit; "adds" (current card 3) implies sums.
- **"Wetness is not real / the water isn't real."** The water is real. Only the feeling can arrive without it. Sources use "perceptual illusion" with "may be" (Neuroscience News: "may be more of a 'perceptual illusion'"), so don't say "wet is an illusion" flat; say the feeling can be made without water.
- **"You can't actually feel wet" (current bold line).** False as written: you do feel wet. Fix in section 8.
- **"A plastic bag does the same as the rubber sheath."** Untested. Bentley used thin rubber, and the ice-cold test was at zero centigrade; nothing was found on plastic bags or on whole hands. Don't tell the reader what will happen to them if they try it.
- **"Touches you all over at once."** No source (claim 6 only supports close and even).
- **"Humans sense humidity."** The Hygroreception page says "It is a sense that is not present in humans." Muggy weather feels sticky through skin touch, heat and sweat, not a humidity sensor. Do not mention muggy air.
- **"Mosquitoes find you by humidity."** Sources say humidity sensors help close up ("nearby hosts"; humidity gradients "appear to act redundantly with heat"), so not the main way and not from far away. Say "close up", never "find you".
- **"Insects have wet sensors" in the same sense.** They sense how damp the air is (water vapour), not the feeling of a wet surface. Say "damp air".
- **Four sensors named in the picture (Cold, Heat, Touch, Pain).** Skin has others (vibration, itch). Say "such as".
- **Clothes off a washing line "can feel damp even when dry."** Plausible, but no source found; see section 8.

## 5. One good number
Water takes heat out of your skin about 25 times faster than air does. Everyday comparison: a room and a swimming pool at the same temperature feel nothing alike; the pool feels far colder because it steals your heat so much faster. **Medium-high** (claim 4): it is the ratio of two measured material properties (0.5918 for water against 0.024-0.025 for air, about 24 times), not a measured rate for a wet fingertip; moving air or water changes it. Say "about 25 times" and "the same temperature". Do not print the old "over 20 times" and "20x" label as a measured skin number; keep it as "much faster" in the picture.
*Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_thermal_conductivities; National Weather Service line via search ("up to 25 times faster than air of the same temperature"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothermia.*

## 6. Everyday comparisons
- **Mixing two paints into a new colour.** Right for the key point: the colour you get is neither of the two, and "wet" is neither "cold" nor "touch". Misleads if the reader thinks the two signals blur into one and disappear, or that the brain "paints" at will; and it implies a fixed recipe, when warm water gives less wet. **Use in one line if at all; say "a new feeling".**
- **Taste and smell making flavour.** Right idea (no sensor for flavour; the brain builds it from two senses). Misleads only through the word "sensor" if the topic also uses it for the tongue. No source fetched for it in this check, so it is untested here: **don't use without a source; a spare for the owner.**
- **A musical chord from two notes.** Right: you hear one thing built from two. Misleads if the reader pictures sound, not skin; and needs no number. **Fine as a one-line alternative, no better than paint.**
- **An optical illusion or a trick image.** Misleads: it suggests the feeling is a mistake or is faked, when it is how wet is always felt, even with real water. **Don't use.**
- **A mask or costume.** Misleads: nothing is hidden underneath. **Don't use.**
Recommendation: use none. The reader needs no analogy: Bentley's finger in a rubber sheath is already the picture. If one is wanted, the paint line.

## 7. Go deeper thread
Some animals do have a sensor for how damp the air is, which people do not. Fruit flies keep humidity sensors in their antennae, and mosquitoes have two kinds on theirs: one that reacts to dry air and one to damp air. Up close the sensors help a mosquito tell a person is near, and the damp-air kind helps it find water to lay eggs in. They sense water vapour in the air, not the feeling of a wet surface.
*high for the facts (claim 13; fruit fly: Curr Biol 2016; mosquito: Neuron 2023, PNAS 2024, Curr Opin Neurobiol 2026). "Close up", not "find you". Alternative thread: menthol (spare 14), the cold sensor can be switched on by a chemical with nothing cold.*

## 8. The old topic's claims: unsafe, hedged, or fine
Fine as is:
- Card 2 "Water pulls heat out of your skin over 20 times faster than air does." True (claim 4); say "about 25" if a number is kept.
- Card 4 core: "your hand stays dry, yet feels wet in cold water; warm feels less wet." Right for a rubber sheath in 1900 (claims 9 and 10). The words "thin plastic bag" and "your skin cools fast and feels that soft, even press" are the issue below.

- **Slide 1 "Take clothes off a washing line on a cold evening. They can feel damp, even when they are dry."** Unsourced. Replace with Bentley's finger (claim 9), or drop the example; the surprise needs one statement, not three.
- **Slide 1 "You have no sense for wet" / "Your skin cannot sense wet at all" / "None of them senses wet" (three statements of the same surprise).** Keep one. Plain true version: "Your skin has no sensor made just for wet." (claim 3).
- **Slide 1 "It has tiny nerve endings, called sensors, for cold, heat, touch and pain."** Defined three times across slides 1-3. Define once on the first slide in one sentence (claim 1, with "such as"), then use the word without redefining.

- **Card 2 "Your skin has tiny nerve endings, called sensors, that notice cold."** Redefinition. Replace: "Your cold sensors notice your skin getting colder." (claim 2.)

- **Card 3 "A liquid touches you gently and evenly, all over at once. It moves and fits every bump of your skin."** "All over at once" and "fits every bump" are unsourced. Replace: "A liquid also gives your skin a close, even touch." (claim 6.)
- **Card 3 "Touch sensors, tiny nerve endings, feel how the liquid presses and moves."** Redefinition, and the 2017 review says which touch nerves carry wet is unknown. Replace: "Your touch sensors feel that close, even touch."

Together, they usually mean a liquid."** "Usually" hedge; "usually mean a liquid" is also unsourced (cold, close touches exist without a liquid; that is the topic). - **Card 4 "Put your hand in a thin plastic bag, and dip it in cold water. Your hand stays dry."** Untested as written (a plastic bag, a whole hand, "cold" not defined); the test is a thin rubber sheath over a finger (claim 9). - **Card 4 "Now try warm water, close to your skin's own heat. ## 9. Replacement words
- **"sensor"**: one word, defined once on the first slide as "tiny nerve endings in your skin that each react to one thing", then used bare: "cold sensors", "touch sensors". Never "receptor", "afferent", "nerve fibre", "tiny nerve endings, called sensors" again.
- **"wet"**: for the feeling; "damp" only in the insect Go-deeper ("damp air"). Never "moist", "moisture", "liquidity", "humid".
- **"cold"** (not "cool", "cooling", "chill") for the signal and the skin: "your skin gets colder", "a cold signal". Avoid "cooling" as a noun.
- **"touch"** (not "press", "pressure", "soft", "gentle") with "close, even" as its one description: "a close, even touch".
- **"puts together"** or **"builds"** (not "adds", "guess", "decides", "integrates", "works out").
- **"signal"** for what a sensor sends; **"your brain"** for who combines.
- **"heat leaves your skin" / "takes heat out of your skin"** (not "conducts", "evaporates", "heat transfer").
- Hedge replacements: "usually cooler than your skin" becomes "water cooler than your skin"; "usually mean a liquid" becomes "a wet thing gives you both"; "does not always say wet" is dropped. Use "some people", "in a lab test", "in 1900" to state the limit of a study as a fact about the study, not as a hedge on the claim.
- **"a thin rubber sheath"** (Bentley's own word, readers know a thin rubber glove); do not write "plastic bag".
