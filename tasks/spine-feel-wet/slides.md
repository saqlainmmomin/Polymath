**Headline:** Can your skin feel wet when it is dry?
**Setup:** Your skin has no sensor made just for wet.

**Headline:** Water cooler than your skin takes heat out of it, and your cold sensors notice
**Text:** When something wet touches you, water cooler than your skin takes heat out of it fast. Your cold sensors notice your skin getting colder and send a cold signal to your brain.
**Hook:** But is a cold signal all it takes to feel wet?

### Slide 3
**Headline:** Your brain puts a cold signal and a close, even touch together into wet
**Text:** A wet thing also gives your skin a close, even touch, and your touch sensors feel it. Your brain puts the cold signal and the touch together into wet.
**Hook:** So could a dry finger feel wet?
**Picture:** The same drop giving the skin a close, even touch, and a touch sensor lights up beside the cold one. The cold signal and the touch signal meet at the brain and join into one word, wet. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** COLD SIGNAL · CLOSE, EVEN TOUCH · TOUCH SENSOR · YOUR BRAIN · WET

### Slide 4
**Headline:** In 1900, a dry finger in a thin rubber sheath felt wet in water
**Text:** So you can feel wet when you are dry. In 1900, a psychologist covered a finger with a thin rubber sheath, like a glove for one finger. The finger was then lowered into water, and it stayed dry but felt wet. The people who tried it at first refused to believe the finger was dry. In the same test, warmer water felt less wet, and lukewarm water barely at all.
**Bold:** Wet is not a sense of its own. Your brain builds it from cold and touch.
**Picture:** A finger in a thin rubber sheath dips into a glass of water. The finger inside stays dry and the word wet appears. Then warmer water: less wet. Then lukewarm: barely. Loops by itself.
**Picture labels:** DRY FINGER · THIN RUBBER SHEATH · WATER · FEELS WET · WARMER: LESS WET · LUKEWARM: BARELY

### Go deeper
Some animals do have a sensor for how damp the air is, which people do not. Fruit flies keep humidity sensors in their antennae, and mosquitoes have two kinds on theirs: one that reacts to dry air and one to damp air. Up close the sensors help a mosquito tell a person is near, and the damp-air kind helps it find water to lay eggs in. They sense water vapour in the air, not the feeling of a wet surface.
