How does your brain decide what to remember? Ever forgotten where you put your keys? Odds are, you never really “lost” that memory. You never made it. Making a memory starts with encoding: turning what you see and hear into a form your brain can keep.

Your senses take in far more than you could ever keep, so attention works like a spotlight. Only what it lands on gets a chance to become a memory. The rest mostly fades.

What you noticed then waits in working memory, a tiny workspace that holds about four things. Once your mind moves on, they slip away in seconds, unless you work with them.

And how you work with them decides how well they stick. Just repeating a phone number leaves a weak memory. Thinking about what something means, and linking it to what you know, leaves a strong one.

You can picture a penny, but could you draw which way the head faces, or where the words sit? Most people can't: they've seen thousands but never really looked. You can't remember what you never took in.
