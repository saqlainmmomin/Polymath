How does a new memory become one that lasts? A memory is a set of brain cells that fired together during an experience, now linked so they can fire together again. But a brand-new memory is like wet cement: it's soft at first, and it needs time to set. So what does it take for a memory to set?

Turning it into a memory that lasts is called consolidation. Within hours, those cells build new proteins, the building material that makes their links sturdier.

A memory has pieces: what you saw, heard and felt, each kept in a different part of your brain. The hippocampus, a seahorse-shaped part deep in your brain, ties those pieces together into one memory. So what does the hippocampus do while you sleep?

In deep sleep, the hippocampus replays your day to the cortex, the brain's outer layer, gradually moving it into long-term storage.

Studies show that students who sleep after studying remember more than students who pull an all-nighter. Learning doesn't end when you close the book. A new memory starts soft, its cells build proteins, the hippocampus ties its pieces together, and sleep moves it into long-term storage.
