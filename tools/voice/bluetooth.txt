How does Bluetooth work? Bluetooth is a short-range radio link: two devices, like a phone and an earbud, send each other data through the air as radio waves. So how do two devices find each other?

To meet, one device announces itself, saying "I'm here", and the other spots it. Then they swap secret codes, called keys. The keys scramble what they send later, and let the two reconnect on their own next time. Once they're connected, they have one more problem to deal with.

Wi-Fi sits on the same band of airwaves as Bluetooth, so the two could collide. To avoid that, Bluetooth hops. It cuts its airwaves into dozens of narrow channels, like the stations on a radio dial, and one Wi-Fi network can cover about a quarter of that dial. So the devices never stay on one channel. Streaming music, they hop as often as 1,600 times a second. Each hop carries a tiny piece of music. If a hop lands on Wi-Fi, only that piece is lost, and it's sent again. If the resend is late, the music skips for a split second. But how do the two devices know which channel to hop to next?

One of the two sets the timing and the order of channels, and the other follows it, hop for hop. That way they always land on the same channel at the same instant. So what kinds of Bluetooth are there?

Bluetooth comes in two flavors. Classic streams without a break, for headphones and speakers. Low Energy sends small bursts and sleeps in between, for fitness trackers and smart tags. Bluetooth is a short-range radio link: two devices introduce themselves once, then hop between channels together to stay clear of Wi-Fi.
