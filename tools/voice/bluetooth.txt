How does Bluetooth work? Bluetooth is a short-range radio link: two devices, like a phone and an earbud, send each other data as radio waves. And it runs on the same stretch of airwaves that lots of Wi-Fi uses, so the two share a crowded space. So how do two devices find each other?

To meet, one device announces itself, saying "I'm here", and the other spots it. Then they swap secret codes, called keys. The keys scramble what they send later, and let the two reconnect on their own next time. So once they're connected, how do they cope with all that Wi-Fi?

Bluetooth cuts its airwaves into dozens of narrow channels, like the stations on a radio dial. One Wi-Fi network can cover about a quarter of that dial. So the devices never stay on one channel. Streaming music, they hop between channels as often as 1,600 times a second. Each hop carries a tiny piece of music. If a hop lands on Wi-Fi, only that piece is lost, and it's sent again. If the resend is late, the music skips for a split second. But for hopping to work, both devices must land on the same channel every time.

Both devices have to be on the same channel at the same instant. So one of them sets the timing and the order of channels, and the other follows it, hop for hop. So what does all that radio cost in battery?

Very little. Bluetooth typically reaches only about 10 meters, at very low power, which is why earbuds and watches run for hours on tiny batteries. It comes in two flavors. Classic streams without a break, for headphones and speakers. Low Energy sends small bursts and sleeps in between, for fitness trackers and smart tags. Bluetooth is a short-range radio link: two devices introduce themselves once, then hop between channels together to stay clear of Wi-Fi.
