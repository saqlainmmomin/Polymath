Why does one Moon give most beaches two high tides a day? That Moon is on one side of the sky at a time, and it pulls the sea toward it, so you'd expect one high tide a day. So how does the Moon's pull make a second high tide?

The Moon pulls harder on whatever is closer. So the sea facing it, the near sea, is pulled harder than the ground under it, while the sea on the far side, the far sea, is pulled less hard than the ground. So where does all that pulling make the sea pile up?

Picture three runners in a line. One magnet ahead pulls them all, the front one hardest and the back one least. The front runner is the near sea, the middle one the ground, the back one the far sea. The near sea is pulled ahead of the ground, and the ground is pulled ahead of the far sea. So the sea piles up on both sides of Earth, in two bulges. So what happens to your beach as Earth turns?

The bulges stay lined up with the Moon while Earth turns once a day beneath them. Your beach passes under one, then the other. That's two high tides, a bit over twelve hours apart. But why does each day's high tide come a little later?

The Moon also moves round Earth the way Earth spins. So each day your beach needs about 50 extra minutes to catch up with it, and the high tide comes that much later. One Moon stretches the sea on both sides of Earth, and Earth turns your beach through both.
