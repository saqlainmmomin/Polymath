How does your brain bring back a memory? Remembering isn't pressing play on a video. A memory is kept as linked pieces, what you saw, heard and felt, stored in different parts of your brain. So what starts bringing those pieces back?

Recall starts with a cue: a question, a smell, a song or a place that matches one of those pieces. So what happens once a cue wakes up one piece?

That piece wakes up, and the signal spreads along the links until the whole memory comes back. And when you can't remember something, the memory is often still there, just missing the right cue. That's the tip-of-the-tongue feeling. So if the pieces are put back together each time, can a memory change?

Because the pieces are put back together each time, your brain fills any gaps with what it knows and expects. So a memory can change without you noticing.

In one famous experiment, people watched a video of a car crash. Some were asked how fast the cars were going when they "hit" each other. Others were asked how fast they were going when they "smashed" into each other. The "smashed" group guessed faster speeds, and some even remembered broken glass that was never there. Remembering is rebuilding, not replaying.
