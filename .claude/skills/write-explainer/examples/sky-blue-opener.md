# Sky-blue slide 1, written by the owner (2026-10-26). The voice bar.

The skill's old rules (15-word sentences, one idea each) produced this instead, which the owner rejected:

> The sky is not really blue. Something else makes it look blue. On a sunny day, the Sun looks yellow. But the sky looks blue. At sunset, the sky looks orange and red. Air has no colour of its own. It is clear, like glass. So where do the blue and the orange come from?

The owner's version:

**Title:** Why is the sky blue at noon and red at sunset?
**Subtitle:** It's the same sky and the same Sun.
**Text:** Sunlight looks yellow, but it's really all colours mixed together. Air looks clear, but it isn't quite. So how does clear air turn sunlight into a blue sky, and later an orange one?

## What it does
- Three sentences of 8 to 16 words, joined with "but" and "so", with contractions.
- Two "looks X, but really Y" pairs. Each plants the next slides: sunlight is every colour (slide 2), air is not quite clear (slide 3).
- Colours are named once or twice. The title and the question carry them.
- The title names two moments (noon, sunset) and is literally true.
- One hook, a mechanism question: "how does A turn into B?".
- No simile, no definition, no false claim ("air has no colour of its own" was wrong).
