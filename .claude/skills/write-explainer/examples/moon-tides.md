# Moon and tides, drafted with this skill (2026-10-25, not built, not fact checked)

The owner asked "How does the Moon influence waves on Earth?". The Moon causes tides, not waves, so the title was changed (title rule: literally true). Last slide clears up the waves myth.

### Slide 1
**Headline:** How does the Moon move the oceans?
**Setup:** Twice a day, the sea comes in and goes out again.
**Text:** Stand on a beach for a few hours. The water slowly creeps up the sand. Later, it slowly slides back down. This rise and fall is called the tide. It happens on every coast on Earth.
**Hook:** The cause is about 384,000 kilometres away, up in the sky.
**Picture:** a beach time-lapse, waterline creeping up and down while a small Moon crosses the sky.

### Slide 2
**Headline:** The Moon pulls on the ocean
**Text:** Everything in space pulls on everything else. This pull is called gravity. The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth. Rock is stiff, so it hardly moves. Water is free to flow. So the ocean slides a little toward the Moon. It makes a bulge of water on the side facing the Moon.
**Hook:** That makes one bulge. But Earth actually has two.

### Slide 3
**Headline:** The ocean bulges on both sides
**Text:** The Moon pulls hardest on the side of Earth closest to it. It pulls less on Earth's middle. It pulls least on the far side. So the near water moves toward the Moon the most. The far water moves the least, so it gets left behind. That makes a second bulge, on the far side.
**Hook:** Two bulges sit still under the Moon. Then Earth spins underneath them.

### Slide 4
**Headline:** Earth spins through both bulges every day
**Text:** Earth turns around once a day. Your beach passes through both bulges as it turns. Inside a bulge, the water is high. This is high tide. Between the bulges, the water is low. This is low tide. So most beaches get two high tides and two low tides each day.
**Hook:** But some days, the high tides are much higher. Something else is pulling too.

### Bridge
The Moon also moves along its path around Earth. So your beach needs a little extra time to catch up with each bulge. That is why high tide comes about 50 minutes later every day.
**Hook:** The Moon is not the only thing in the sky that pulls on the sea.

### Slide 5
**Headline:** The Sun pulls on the oceans too
**Text:** The Sun is huge, but it is very far away. Its pull on the tides is a bit less than half the Moon's. At full moon and new moon, the Sun and Moon line up. Their pulls add together, and tides get bigger. These are called spring tides. They have nothing to do with the season.
**Hook:** Even so, the bulge is small out at sea. So why do some beaches get giant tides?

### Slide 6
**Headline:** The shape of the coast makes tides bigger
**Text:** Out in the open ocean, the bulge is less than a metre high. Near land, the coast changes it. A long, narrow bay squeezes the water like a funnel. The water piles up higher and higher. In Canada's Bay of Fundy, the sea rises and falls about 16 metres. That is taller than a four-storey building.
**Closing line (bold):** The Moon doesn't make waves. Wind does. The Moon slowly lifts the whole ocean, twice a day.

### Go deeper
The Moon pulls on rock too. Twice a day, the ground under your feet rises and falls by about 30 centimetres. You never feel it, because everything around you moves with it.
